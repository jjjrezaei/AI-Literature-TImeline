
<p>Released in 2022, <i>Cyberpunk: Edgerunners</i> is an anime released by Netflix that expands the universe of its video game predecessor, <i>Cyberpunk 2077</i> through a tightly focused narrative about class, embodiment, and technological dependence. Set in Night City, a metropolis governed by corporate power, algorithmic surveillance, and ubiquitous cybernetic enhancement, the series follows David Martinez, a teenager whose adoption of military‑grade implants after the tragic death of his mother promises empowerment but ultimately subjects him to machinic logics beyond his control. In <i>Cyberpunk: Edgerunners</i>, the escalating integration of cybernetic augmentation—especially David’s use of the Sandevistan cybernetic implant—reveals how AI operates not as a discrete, sentient being but as an infrastructural force that reorganizes human identity and agency. By depicting machinic systems gradually overriding David’s bodily autonomy and decision-making, the series exposes the instability of the human subject in technologically saturated environments. This challenges linear narratives of AI “progress” by showing that cultural anxieties about human‑machine entanglement long predate contemporary generative AI, reframing AI as a historical continuum of fears about autonomy, embodiment, and control.</p><p>Cyberpunk as a genre has long interrogated the permeable boundary between human and machine. Narratives like Mamoru Oshii’s <i>Ghost in the Shell</i> imagine worlds where bodies are sites of technological intervention and where AI is embedded in everyday infrastructures rather than isolated in humanoid robots. These stories foreground the social, economic, and political conditions that shape technological development, emphasizing how systems of power—not just machines themselves—govern human life.</p><p><i>Cyberpunk: Edgerunners</i> inherits this lineage. David’s main cybernetic enhancement, the Sandevistan, is a spinal implant that accelerates the user’s perception and movement to superhuman levels. While not an AI in the anthropomorphic sense, the Sandevistan embodies algorithmic logic by optimizing, calculating, and executing actions faster than conscious thought. Its operation depends on predictive processing and automated decision‑making—qualities associated with contemporary AI systems. The implant becomes a crucial lens for examining how machinic infrastructures infiltrate human embodiment, raising questions about autonomy, identity, and the limits of the self.</p><p>A pivotal moment in the series occurs in Episode 9, when David repeatedly activates the Sandevistan despite warnings that his body is nearing collapse. The animation fractures into jittery, stuttering frames, visually mimicking a machine pushed beyond its operational limits. Mechanical whirring overlays David’s breathing, creating a hybrid sonic texture that blurs the line between human and device. His movements become instantaneous and algorithmic—less the product of conscious intention than of machinic optimization. The Sandevistan does not “take over” in a dramatic, sentient‑AI sense; instead, it subtly reconfigures his perception and decision‑making until the distinction between his autonomy and the implant’s logic becomes impossible to parse. The scene literalizes the fear that technological systems can subsume human autonomy not through rebellion, but through integration.</p><p>N. Katherine Hayles’ theory of the posthuman provides a critical framework for understanding this transformation. In <i>How We Became Posthuman</i>, Hayles argues that the posthuman subject emerges when the body is reconceived as “human identity is essentially an informational pattern rather than an embodied enaction.” (Hayles xii). David’s experience exemplifies this shift: his identity becomes inseparable from the flows of data and algorithmic processing coursing through the Sandevistan. His body is no longer a stable, self‑determining entity but a platform for machinic optimization.</p><p>Adding <i>Cyberpunk: Edgerunners</i> to our AI timeline challenges the assumption that AI history is a linear progression toward increasingly intelligent machines, as it reframes AI as a social and infrastructural condition, embedded in systems that shape human behavior, embodiment, and identity. By foregrounding AI as a force that reorganizes human life rather than merely a technological artifact, Edgerunners underscores that anxieties about autonomy, embodiment, and machinic control have deep cultural roots—making it a vital addition to any critical, historically informed AI timeline.</p><p>Works Cited</p><p>Hayles, N.
