# "The Poetry Cloud" by Cixin Liu

<p>As artificial intelligence begins to rapidly expand and evolve, a multitude of inquiries have come into fruition. Questions regarding ethics of development, environmental impacts, and full transparency of capability have all manifested at some stage in the discussions held. Beyond these ethical and technological concerns, another voice of argument has arisen to challenge the intersection and convergence between modern day A.I. and cultural production. This voice anticipates and inherently questions the validity of A.I. 's participation in the humanities and artistic realms, primarily focusing on A.I.’s “authorship” of literature. Within this team of voices, or bucket of crabs* Cixin Liu, the author of “The Poetry Cloud” emerges with an imperative, science-fiction narrative that offers fantastical perspective on the discourse surrounding the convergence of A.I. and literary production. Published in March 2003 within an issue of Science Fiction World, “The Poetry Cloud” fixates its story on the interaction of three primary characters, Yiyi (a human scholar), Big-Tooth (a dinosaur) and a ‘god’ whom reconstructs themselves as the former Tang dynasty poet Li Bai. Through their interactions, this technologically omnipotent ‘god’ develops a fascination with human poetry and seeks to create the ultimate poem surpassing the talent of Li Bai and human kind in general. Ultimately, this entity of technological omnipotence becomes so infatuated with this ambition that it consumes all of the solar system’s energy to develop an A.I. cloud program. The fantastical realities of this A.I, cloud program are that it converts all possible Chinese character combinations to ‘author’ the ultimate poem and thus develops the aforementioned poetry cloud. This highly imaginative storyline, from the complexities of the universe to the omnipotence of the ‘god’ reconstruction of Li Bai, functions as an imperative conversation piece regarding the validity of A.I. authorship. The “Poetry Cloud” by Cixin Liu merits an official spot on the cumulative timeline of A.I. due to its narrative implications that challenge the validity of A.I. authorship in literature.<p>

<p>For the purposes of this discussion, the concept of "validity" when referring to A.I. authorship largely pertains to whether A.I. authorship is convincingly sincere in its language exploring human experiences or whether it remains highly mimetic and insincere. “The Poetry Cloud” by Cixin Liu challenges the notion that A.I. authorship is valid largely because of the inability for A.I. to truly experience the nuance behind our emotionally driven life cycles. Within the narrative, the character Yiyi expresses the following sentiment when addressing the technologically omnipotent god, “Impossible. For one thing, you are not human, and cannot feel human passions. Human art is to you merely a flower carved in stone, and you cannot overcome this obstacle with technology,” (Liu, 9). This sentiment illustrates one of the foundational arguments Liu delivers within this narrative: that A.I. and technology lack valid authorship due to their inability to thoroughly experience what it means to be human. The language behind the metaphor of human art being comparable to a flower carved in stone furthers this argument as it degrades human passion to something stagnant and static. Technology and A.I. lack the capability to understand nuance and fluidity behind human passion. Liu’s foundational argument is a sentiment many share and is evident in Foteini Dimirouli’s article “AI Poetry and the Human Writing Subject” published in February of 2024. Dimirouli’s article argues that A.I. is becoming increasingly capable of generating convincing poetry and in turn this capability is creating inquiries about whether or not the ‘human’ part of authorship still matters. In Dimirouli’s article, the following statement was made, “Even when poets are anonymous, or unknown, there is a sense of a shared understanding of joy and suffering that does not merely reside in words, but also operates upon assumptions about the writer’s humanity. Even if language is an imperfect medium through which to truly understand the pain of the other, we still know that there is something universal about, for example, being heartbroken, which has been largely communicated through an empathetic bond between cultural consumer and creator, not just in poetry, but also in music or film,” (Dimirouli). This excerpt offers an external perspective that intertwines elegantly with the foundational argument made by Cixin Liu. The specific wording of “...sense of shared understanding of joy and suffering that does not merely reside in words but also operates upon assumptions about the writer’s humanity,” (Dimiroulli) insightfully acknowledges the importance of human experience within literature. Furthermore, this acknowledgement sets the expectation for validity in authorship. The “empathetic bond” is something that A.I. cannot reconstruct authentically or sincerely in the manner necessary for valid authorship in literature. Ultimately, Dimirouli and Liu both offer suggestions that challenge the validity of A.I. authorship and argue that without lived human experience or an empathetic bond to its reader, A.I. generated literature remains mimetic and insincere. <p>

<p>“The Poetry Cloud” by Cixin Liu merits an official spot on the cumulative timeline of A.I. development and impact due to its important narrative implications surrounding the validity of A.I. authorship. This short story offers imperative, philosophical suggestions surrounding how technology and A.I. are unable to fully conceptualize the nuance of the human experience and connect it to the insincerity of its production of literature. Through the exploration of validity and how it pertains to A.I. authorship, this discussion revealed that the ability to form “empathetic bonds” and experience human emotional processes and contexts are crucial to the production of literature. Furthermore, Cixin Liu’s narrative illustrates the mimetic nature of A.I. literature through his fantastical creation of the A.I. based poetry cloud. Despite being able to mimic the technique and approximate structure, the production of poems within this poetry cloud lack the lived human experience they aim to represent. This creates dissonance and a lack of true, empathetic bond between reader and author that literature requires. The connection of Cixin Liu’s narrative implications and Foteini Dimirouli’s observations surrounding the reception of poetry and literature as a whole offer key analytical points for why “The Poetry Cloud” by Liu deserves to be on this timeline. In conclusion, “The Poetry Cloud” by Cixin Liu merits a permanent position on this timeline as it raises and argues against the validity of A.I. authorship and promotes continuity of authenticity when representing human culture. <p>
