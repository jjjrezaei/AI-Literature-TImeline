# <i>Galatea by Emily Short (2000): When Art Asked the Questions Science Couldn't</i>

<p>When we think about AI as a whole, as a history, we usually think of and envision 
laboratories, computer scientists, and technical breakthroughs where they throw papers 
in the air and scream in excitement. We rarely think about a marble statue standing 
alone in a gallery, waiting to be spoken to. With <i>Galatea</i> though, Emily Short's 
interactive fiction game released in 2000, belongs on any serious timeline of AI. Not 
because it is a work of art and literature, but most precisely because of it. 
<i>Galatea</i> disrupts the assumption that progress in AI thinking only happens in 
technical spaces, and it forces us to ask the questions that actually matter. Not just 
"can a machine think?" but <b>"how do we decide when something is worth listening to?"</b></p>

<i>Galatea</i> is a single-room, single-character text-based game in which the player 
converses with a living marble statue which is Galatea, the figure from the Greek myth 
of Pygmalion. There are zero puzzles, a game in which there is no winning in the 
traditional sense. The entire game is just having a conversation. According to Short's 
own description of the piece, <i>Galatea</i> "has a multilinear plot" with "a large 
number of endings" that are driven entirely by the choices made in the dialogue, and 
the game tracks the state of conversation to determine how Galatea responds. The way 
this was designed is not accidental at all, it is the argument. Short built a character 
whose intelligence is demonstrated entirely through conversation, which is exactly the 
framework Alan [[Turing]] proposed fifty years earlier.

<p>In his essay from 1950 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," Turing famously replaced 
the question of "Can machines think?" with a behavioral one he called the 
<b>Imitation Game</b>. In which he asked, could a machine, communicating only through 
text, respond alike from a human? Turing argued that if a machine could hold its own 
in open-ended conversation, we would have no reasonable grounds to deny its intelligence 
(Turing, 50). <i>Galatea</i> essentially stages this test from the other side. We are 
the interrogators, and Galatea is the subject. But Short really complicates the 
experiment in a way Turing did not fully anticipate. That Galatea does not just respond, 
she initiates, she gets frustrated, she circles back to things that you have said earlier 
without being prompted to, and she grieves. At one point during my play through, after 
the player asks about food and moves on, Galatea volunteers unprompted, "I've never even 
seen anything I wanted to eat. It seems a disgusting process, to be honest." That wasn't 
just any response, that was a continuation of thought.</p>

<p>This directly challenges what Turing called the <b>Lady Lovelace Objection</b>, in which 
the idea that a machine "can only do what we know how to order it to perform" and 
therefore can never truly originate anything (Turing, 59). Short's design pushes back 
on this really hard. Whether or not Galatea's behavior is "really" original in a 
philosophical sense, it functions like originality. It surprises, it exceeds the prompt.</p>

<p>What makes <i>Galatea</i> as a whole so important on this timeline is that it shows a 
humanist tradition of grappling with AI questions at the same depth as the scientific 
one and doing so through experience rather than an argument. Turing wrote about machine 
intelligence for philosophers in a journal, while Short made players feel the problem 
in their gut. Both of these matter so much. A timeline that only includes the technical 
milestones misses half the conversation that AI has already been embedded in — the one 
about <b>what it means to recognize a mind at all.</b></p>
