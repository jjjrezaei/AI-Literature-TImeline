
[[Cartesian dualism]], as articulated by René Descartes, reads more as an exposé than a doctrine; yet, despite his insistence that it be “accept[ed]…as the first principle of the philosophy of which [he] was in search” (Descartes, Part IV), this evolving inquiry was ultimately received as a fixed system rather than a point of departure—something to be continually questioned and refined. Writing in the midst of the Scientific Revolution—alongside figures like Galileo and, later, Newton—Descartes emerged at a moment when inherited truths were being challenged and intellectual authority was shifting toward reason, scientific inquiry, and skepticism. Here, his grappling with thought and humanity provided a foundation—a leg to stand on—at a time when questions of creation prompted people to seek new explanations for existence. Yet what begins as an exploratory philosophical project gradually becomes a framework that privileges the mind over the body.
In The Island of Dr. Moreau, H.G. Wells exposes the danger of accepting this separation at face value. Here, we see how Cartesian dualism can impose a disembodied way of being. In this disembodiment, it becomes easier for oppression to occur, as individuals are reduced to vessels—entities meant to perform labor, whether physical or “logical,” without engaging the bodily sensations that accompany action in the world. Moreau describes “moral education” as “an artificial modification and perversion of instinct,” reframing morality not as refinement but as distortion. What society names virtue becomes a form of suppression. If instinct can be rewritten, then morality is not inherent but constructed—something trained into obedience rather than arising naturally.
Of course, there are advantages to separating the mind and body in the name of science, as it allows for analysis on a micro level. However, this perspective can also remove the organism from its context. For example, examining a plant at the cellular level may yield useful insights, but it strips the plant of its relationship to itself and its surrounding environment. Thus, philosophy surpasses science in its own right because it questions the very constructs upon which science depends. Descartes’ project belongs to this philosophical mode—an open-ended inquiry—yet Wells shows what happens when such inquiry is transformed into rigid systems of control.
This transformation becomes especially visible in the role of faith on the island. When Prendick tells the Beast People, “For a time you will not see him… there… where he can watch you… Fear the Law” (Wells 123), he elevates Moreau into a god-like figure whose authority persists even in absence. Here, belief is strategic—constructed to maintain order rather than grounded in truth. Faith becomes another mechanism of control, reinforcing obedience through the illusion of an unseen authority.
Yet this system does not disappear; it follows Prendick beyond the island. Upon returning to society, he finds little difference between the Beast People and humans, observing faces that seem “keen and bright,” yet also “dull or dangerous… unsteady, insincere,” and admits, “I feel as though the animal was surging up through them” (Wells 156). Though he recognizes this perception as an “illusion,” his body still “shrinks” from others, revealing a tension between constructed belief and embodied response. His doubt is no longer directed outward at the island alone, but inward—toward the very distinction between human and animal itself.
In this moment, Prendick confronts the instability of the systems he once relied upon. If the boundary between human and animal cannot be trusted, then neither can the structures built upon it. He turns instead to something less defined, concluding, “There it must be… in the vast and eternal laws of matter… that whatever is more than animal within us must find its solace and its hope… I hope, or I could not live” (Wells 157). Here, hope no longer rests on imposed authority or constructed belief, but on something ambiguous and uncertain—something that cannot be fully named or secured.
Wells reminds us that even our foundations—our systems, our beliefs, and even our hopes—must remain open to doubt, not because they disappear, but because they persist in forms we may fail to recognize. This dynamic extends into contemporary systems such as artificial intelligence, where the separation of mind from body reaches a new extreme. AI operates as a form of disembodied reasoning—processing logic without sensation, context, or lived experience. Like Moreau’s experiments, such systems can reshape behavior, categorize individuals, and define standards of “good” or “acceptable” conduct through constructed frameworks. In this way, the logic of Cartesian dualism persists, not as philosophical inquiry, but as technological structure. As with Wells’ warning, the danger lies not in these systems themselves, but in accepting them without question—placing faith in their authority without recognizing their constructed nature.
