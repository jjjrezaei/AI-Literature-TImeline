In 1843, Ada Lovelace developed what is considered to be the first algorithm designed to be executed by a machine in her notes accompanying a translation of Luigi Menabrea's article on Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine. Although the Analytical Engine was never built, Lovelace's contribution went far beyond the mere translation of the article. Lovelace described a step-by-step procedure to calculate Bernoulli numbers, which is considered to be the first computer program. More importantly, Lovelace described a vision of computation that went beyond the mere computation of numbers to the manipulation of symbols, which would allow the machine to compose music or create images if the rules for the creation of such images were programmed. This is the early beginning of the concept of artificial intelligence, as computation is viewed as the processing of symbols rather than the mere computation of numbers. Lovelace also argued that the machine was unable to create ideas on its own, which is now called the “Lovelace Objection.” Thinkers such as Alan [[Turing]] would later challenge and expand upon these ideas, but Lovelace’s work already contains the central tensions that define AI discourse.                                                                 It is significant to place Lovelace in AI history because it subverts the common perception of artificial intelligence as a relatively new concept related to contemporary digital technologies. Lovelace’s contributions to the field of computer science illustrate that the intellectual foundations of AI, including the issues of symbolic reasoning, mechanized thought, and creativity, were being explored as early as the 19th century, long before the advent of electronic computers. Such a discovery challenges the traditional perception of linear technological progress by demonstrating the possibility of significant ideas being developed independently of the technologies used to implement them. Although the Analytical Engine was never actually constructed, it led to one of the most important conceptual inventions in computer science history. Therefore, it is more appropriate to see the history of AI not as an innovation but as a progression of human attempts to codify thought, and Lovelace’s contributions to it remain highly relevant to contemporary symbolic AI and computational creativity.                                      The inclusion of this entry, therefore, ultimately serves to resituate the development and concept of artificial intelligence as a profoundly historical and theoretical pursuit rather than a modern technological one. It serves to challenge the idea that there was a dramatic break from the past with the development of the modern concept of artificial intelligence and, instead, reveals a continuum of inquiry into the nature of intelligence, creativity, and thought. Lovelace's dual assertions, therefore, continue to influence contemporary discussions regarding the potential for machine learning, authorship, and autonomy. The inclusion of Lovelace's work, therefore, serves to underscore the fact that what is considered new and innovative regarding the development and application of artificial intelligence, particularly with respect to the ability to create written and artistic content, was conceived nearly two hundred years ago.
