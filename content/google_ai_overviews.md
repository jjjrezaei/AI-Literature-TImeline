# Google AI Overviews

<h1>Limited Tokens: Google AI Overviews</h1><p style="text-indent: 50px;">Previously, you had the option to interact with AI, but Google’s AI overview forces us to interact with AI making it the new default, as well as making it hard to get rid of (there is no real way to turn it off that I could find that wasn’t just bypassing it or some other convoluted way), and in doing so is asking us to trust them and their AI. It is the new default. In an almost seamlessly invisible way they have integrated into their search engine this AI function, and in doing so have fundamentally changed how the search function works, and by the AI doing the work for you. By the search being rolled out without really any warning, unless you follow what Google as a company is doing, it feels like it just popped up one day almost as if it’s always been there. It fundamentally changes how we interact with AI, and its aesthetics make it easy for us to fall into this sense of security, and the repeated exposure normalizes its existence and use. I will not say that it is necessarily a completely bad thing, and can be useful especially because it does make things easier in a way, but that does not mean we should trust it implicitly as Google seems to want us to do. In fact, this should prompt us to continue thinking critically and determine if the feature can be fully trusted or not. Because if we start to fully rely on AI like Google seems to be prompting us to do without question, then we lose vital parts of being human: thinking critically, deciding for ourselves, and introspection. So, then, it is important to understand where its strengths lie and its weaknesses as well as how it works because this will allow us to responsibly engage with the AI overview. It also uncovers even more urgently how important some sort of AI literacy education is necessary for the whole population that engages with AI (much like media literacy), after all when one is not educated on something such as AI our imaginations tend to run wild and fear can take over.</p><p style="text-indent: 50px;">First, what even is Google AI Overviews? Simply put they are AI-generated summaries put above the classical search results of some queries (Paz and Seitz 2). Now Google’s AI Overviews rely on their Gemini LLM (Large Language Model) (Paz and Seitz 2). Jim Beggs, previous Technical Director of AI at General Atomics, explains what LLMs are and how they work, </p><p style="margin-left:100px; margin-right:50px;">“The LLM is a machine learning model which is a highly parameterized function which learns the probability of the next token (aka word) based upon the chosen data set. They are good at learning the structure of language from a large corpus like the internet. The prompt is fed into the function and then the most probable next word comes out of the function, goes back through the LLM as another prompt, and this cycle continues until a sufficient answer is formed. An interesting feature of an LLM is that it will always answer a prompt even if the answer is wrong, since it effectively extrapolates, but since it is providing an answer not in its training data (hasn’t seen that specific sequence of words) the probabilities used to form the answer may result in an incorrect answer. Really an LLMs understanding of truth is just a mathematical model of truth, based upon the data seen during training, without any way of fact checking the answer. LLMs lack the basics of introspection, and the ability to research an answer to verify its truth like human fact checkers are able to do. LLMs can’t generalize to make new connections like humans, when its missing datapoints it interpolates. Unlike people—it does not have critical thinking skills—in fact, it has no concept of critical thinking.”</p>Many people do not understand this is what AI overviews is run on, if they did their engagement style with it might be different. They would be more likely to look at the links provided to double-check the AI rather than just blindly trusting it even if it’s just a quick search. They might not critically think quite as much as with the original search function, but still would engage with the AI in a more critical capacity. People overall would be more skeptical. In a way Prendick was right when he said, “it takes a real man to tell a lie” (Wells 120). Because AI does not even understand the concept of a lie, it therefore cannot lie to us, yet it can be wrong. It is the lack of comprehension of concepts such as these that separate AI from humans no matter how amazing it seems.<p style="text-indent: 50px;">Next, let’s move on to understanding how Google gets you to trust them, and thus not question them as much. (Just as a quick aside, Google itself is already perceived as trustworthy just by the fact that it is the main big search engine, and in a way the authority. Their credibility from the past is a big influence on their credibility today, although I do not believe it is as intense. Which also brings in the aspect of power Google has as one of the main authorities of the sphere.) In our class conversations aesthetics were a central theme. Within <i>Frankenstein</i> by Mary Shelley, and <i>The Island of Dr. Moreau</i> by H. G. Wells, it was Victorian aesthetics and for Google it is (what I will dub) transparent aesthetics. These transparent aesthetics are what Google wants to present in order for you to perceive it in a certain way. (As a side note, it also makes me think about how the two aforementioned novels are found manuscripts, which is something I had to keep reminding myself of because if I didn’t, then I could get lost in the story without questioning the reliability of the narrator. It’s the same issue with Google’s transparent aesthetics.) </p> <a href="https://drive.google.com/drive-viewer/AKGpihYvxwdee2UCR0FGo1jYGfKi0FyrvHrubntzUDMWtLTMyy2eDdALu4RmY4T6UPhAKbiCzkUh7M_1eWYatVezfPY_fTDvWyyPtSY=s1600-rw-v1?source=screenshot.guru"> <img src="https://drive.google.com/drive-viewer/AKGpihYvxwdee2UCR0FGo1jYGfKi0FyrvHrubntzUDMWtLTMyy2eDdALu4RmY4T6UPhAKbiCzkUh7M_1eWYatVezfPY_fTDvWyyPtSY=s1600-rw-v1" /> </a><p style="text-indent: 50px;">As you can see from the picture above, Google’s interface is very straightforward and simple. The main focus is on the search bar itself, with other information residing on the edges out of the way. This interface says ‘I’m here for you to search, and it will be easy to do so.’ This creates trust and transparency because of its simplicity and understandability. It’s very intuitive. Now before AI overviews were introduced, once you searched, Google came up with a list of links divided into pages. But now when you input your question you get something more like the next image.</p><a href="https://drive.google.com/drive-viewer/AKGpihYofuQjDHqSJyjoaa02p1T5JBrFxtJ_w4RFcyOswmIL69_ufxcKov4NW26LZDRCszZr2tXVZzAFlP_XnocWTmMlhtPxPlPlMw=s1600-rw-v1?source=screenshot.guru"> <img src="https://drive.google.com/drive-viewer/AKGpihYofuQjDHqSJyjoaa02p1T5JBrFxtJ_w4RFcyOswmIL69_ufxcKov4NW26LZDRCszZr2tXVZzAFlP_XnocWTmMlhtPxPlPlMw=s1600-rw-v1" /> </a><p style="text-indent: 50px;">While AI Overviews now take up a majority of what people first see, the integration with elements we already know—consistent font, same simple background, and even some links on the side—makes it easier for us to accept the new function. On top of that, the language the AI uses is also straightforward and fact-like. It acts like a summary that a teacher would give, and makes it digestible but still professional (no slang, appears to be ‘straight facts,’ very logical, not emotional, etc). This language is something we are used to seeing from trustworthy people, or those who wish to inspire confidence within their image. But there are also intricacies that the AI overview does not account for, such as using sources that incorporate pay-walls, so even if you click on the link, an individual cannot confirm nor read the information in context. Overall the AI sounds confident, but when you press the “show more” button something interesting pops up.</p><a href="https://drive.google.com/drive-viewer/AKGpihYVAgI8I6lDnaH-RBpmH3pbtVL7GzyUQbPKFP0hKhvn5RddmRGdnreixxoXcV7QkZy7bYWvd7sICYjFpMDfIbAZ2M1z5tgyQh4=s1600-rw-v1?source=screenshot.guru"> <img src="https://drive.google.com/drive-viewer/AKGpihYVAgI8I6lDnaH-RBpmH3pbtVL7GzyUQbPKFP0hKhvn5RddmRGdnreixxoXcV7QkZy7bYWvd7sICYjFpMDfIbAZ2M1z5tgyQh4=s1600-rw-v1" /> </a><p style="text-indent: 50px;"><b>“AI responses may include mistakes,”</b> in tiny print only visible if you actually look for it. If people are trying to do a quick search, then by making the ‘disclaimer’ look like this, and be in the position it is, people are less likely to press the show more button as the main answer is given within the very first chunk visible. In fact a pew research study states that, “Google users who encountered an AI summary also rarely clicked on a link in the summary itself. This occurred in just 1% of all visits to pages with such a summary” (Chapekis and Lieb)  It’s very similar to both the beast people in <i>The Island of Dr. Moreau</i>, and the creature in <i>Frankenstein</i> where the creators are trying to make them as familiar as possible, thus manipulating the aesthetics to serve a certain purpose. Victor, “selected his features as beautiful,” and while it ended up not going as he had planned, it still was his intention regardless of the outcome (Shelley 47). Victor is familiar with beauty in the sense of Victorian aesthetics, just like how Google is familiar with the digital aesthetics of today, except Google is much more successful because instead of trying to make it too different they make the AI overviews flawlessly familiar (in an almost uncanny way).</p><p style="text-indent: 50px;">Another way Google gets people to trust them is their use of language. While it was mentioned a bit above as aesthetics, it is important to expand on language when it comes to AI overall: “That’s why we as a company consider it an imperative to pursue AI responsibly. We are committed to leading and setting the standard in developing and shipping useful and beneficial applications, applying ethical principles grounded in human values” (Manyika, et al). The main words here being “responsibly,” standard,” “beneficial,” and “ethical principles.” All of the aforementioned words have a positive connotation and are used to ensure the reader will see this statement as a sort of creed Google will abide by when dealing with AI. A creed is in its language just like with Moreau’s laws, “‘<i>His</i> is the House of Pain. <i>His</i> is the House that makes. <i>His</i> is the Hand that wounds. <i>His</i> is the House that heals’” (Wells 59). Most of the language used here focuses on mixing pain and pleasure, with an overwhelming sense of fear. But we also know that this creed is broken multiple times by the beast folk, and the same could be said about Google. A concerning example from January of this year was an investigation done by <i>The Guardian</i> that found Google’s AI Overview contained inaccurate medical advice such as “incorrectly list[ing] a Pap test as a diagnostic tool” to diagnose vaginal cancer (Ticong et al.). This is an obvious breach of their creed, because if they really were thinking of ethical principles, then why wouldn’t they think about people querying for medical advice? There are probably many people who work on AI, and not one of them brought up the possibility? Maybe some did, and Google just chose not to listen, but I digress. </p><p style="text-indent: 50px;"> It’s easy for us, as humans, to fall for targeted aesthetics like these, especially when it comes from a mostly reliable source that used to be very credible to the average person. And when AI is already so mysterious to most, it makes it even harder to think critically about situations such as Google has created. It is when things seem invisible or transparent that we either skip over them or trust them. If you are never taught how to consistently notice these things, then those aspects make it hard to stop for a moment and dig deeper. So by looking at examples such as Google’s AI overviews, we can become more attuned to actually thinking critically about AI rather than just saying it is good or bad, thus becoming more AI literate.</p><h2>Works Cited</h2>
