# The Island of Dr. Moreau

H.G. Wells published the novel <i>The Island of Dr. Moreau</i> in 1896, which explores the violent creation of animal-human hybrids through scientific experimentation. Over fifty years later, Alan Turing proposed the [[Turing]] Test in his 1950 essay “Computing Machinery and Intelligence.” Turing’s essay emerged at a time when early computers were mainly seen as calculating machines rather than entities capable of language or thought. His work was groundbreaking because it shifted the question of machine intelligence from internal consciousness to observable communication, laying the foundation for modern discussions of artificial intelligence. While these works come from very different technological contexts—biological manipulation versus digital computers—they raise the same fundamental questions about what defines the human and whether it can be artificially created. 
A key moment in Wells’s novel occurs when the Beast People recite the repeated Law: “Not to go on all Fours; that is the Law. Are we not Men?” (Wells 69). This ritual shows that humanity is not natural for these creatures but something enforced through language, discipline, and repetition. Their identity depends on consistently performing human behavior through language. The phrase “Are we not Men?” is not a factual statement but a desperate attempt to find self-identity. By insisting that beings must perform humanity through language, Wells challenges the idea of humanity as a natural state, instead suggesting it is something learned, rehearsed, and socially enforced. This concept becomes more significant when compared to Turing’s redefinition of intelligence with the Turing Test. In “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” Turing avoids answering the question “Can machines think?” and instead proposes the Turing Test to see if a machine can successfully imitate human responses in conversation. He states, “The original question, 'Can machines think?’ I believe it to be too meaningless to deserve discussion. Nevertheless, I believe that at the end of the century the use of words and general educated opinion will have altered so much that one will be able to speak of machines thinking without expecting to be contradicted.” (Turing 55) The key issue here is not whether the machine truly thinks but whether it can perform thinking convincingly enough to be indistinguishable from a human. By challenging the question, Turing defines intelligence as something observable and external, based on behavior and language rather than internal consciousness. 
There are parallels between Wells and Turing. Just as the Beast People must repeatedly demonstrate their humanity through the Law, Turing’s machines must showcase intelligence through language. In both cases, identity is judged not by inner consciousness but by observable behavior. However, with Wells, tension arises as the Beast People’s humanity becomes unstable. Despite their efforts, they gradually revert to their animal instincts, suggesting that imitation alone cannot fully sustain human identity. This raises questions when applied to Turing’s framework: if intelligence is defined solely through performance, does it truly mean to think? Although Turing anticipates this concern with his discussion of the “argument from consciousness,” which asserts that machines can’t think because they lack emotional experiences, critics argue that a machine needs to produce something like a sonnet from genuine feelings to be truly intelligent. In this example, Turing presents a hypothetical exchange where a machine provides a thoughtful response to questions about poetry and its meaning. He then asks whether these responses can be dismissed as mechanical or if they compel us to rethink our definition of intelligence. His argument suggests that there's no reliable way to distinguish genuine thought from imitation if both produce identical observable results. This insight underscores the instability already present with the Beast People. They can speak, follow rules, and mimic social behavior, yet their humanity remains uncertain. Similarly, a machine that passes the Turing Test may succeed in imitating human conversation without possessing consciousness. In both cases, imitation reveals the limits of defining humanity or intelligence solely through external signs.
Including these texts on the AI timeline challenges the idea that AI is purely a modern invention. Wells’ novel demonstrates that concerns about artificial beings, imitation, and the boundaries of humanity have existed long before the invention of computers, and Turing’s work formalizes these worries into testable frameworks. The Turing Test marks a turning point that transforms long-standing philosophical questions into a modern computational model. Rather than signaling a complete technological rupture, the shift from biological experimentation to digital simulation continues the same underlying challenge: how to define humanity and intelligence when both can be imitated through external performance.
Ultimately, both Wells and Turing show that humanity and intelligence are not fixed categories but unstable constructs shaped by performance and perception. If something can convincingly act like a human, speak like a human, and respond like a human, then the boundary between the human and the artificial becomes increasingly ambiguous. What seems like a modern technological issue is part of a much longer history of questioning what it truly means to be human.
