# <i>Carole and Tuesday</i>

<i>Carole and Tuesday</i> is an anime series directed by Shinichirō Watanabe, released on April 10, 2019, first on Fuji TV and later distributed internationally through Netflix. Set on a colonized Mars where most culture is produced and consumed through artificial intelligence, the series follows two young women. Carole, a struggling part-time worker with dreams of becoming a musician, and Tuesday, a wealthy girl whose musical aspirations are not taken seriously by those around her. The two meet by chance and decide to pursue their shared dream together, forming a music duo. What makes the show's use of AI particularly striking is that it is embedded in something deeply human and intimate, music. In the world of <i>Carole and Tuesday</i>, all music is created by AI. Down to the tunes, the vocals, and even the lyrics. Artists pay AI systems to generate their music and then perform it for audiences who accept this arrangement as completely normal.
</p>

<p>
The show was released at a significant moment in the real-world development of AI music tools. Platforms and research labs were actively developing AI systems capable of composing, producing, and even mimicking human vocal styles with minimal human input. <i>Carole and Tuesday</i> came about as one of the first major cultural media not merely to imagine AI creativity as a future possibility, but to dramatize in a way what it would feel like to already be living inside a world where that creativity had become the norm. And to quietly ask whether anyone would even notice what had been lost.
</p>

<p>
The central reason <i>Carole and Tuesday</i> is significant to the history of AI is the way it gives cultural and emotional form to a problem that is difficult to articulate in a pure technical term, the uncanny quality of AI-generated creative work. In Sigmund Freud's foundational 1919 essay <i>The Uncanny</i>, the uncanny is described as belonging to "all that arouses dread and creeping horror," a type of category defined not by outright strangeness but by a specific kind of disturbing familiarity, something that seems like it should be recognizable but is wrong in a way that is hard to name (Freud 1). It is, in Freud's framing, the class of the terrifying that leads back to something with longing familiar.
</p>

<p>
This quality appears earlier in the literary tradition that <i>Carole and Tuesday</i> implicitly engage. In H.G. Wells's <i>The Island of Doctor Moreau</i>, the narrator Prendick describes his encounter with the Beast Folk in terms that map precisely onto Freud's later concept. "Suddenly, as I watched their grotesque and unaccountable gestures, I perceived clearly for the first time what it was that had offended me, what had given me the two inconsistent and conflicting impressions of utter strangeness and yet of the strangest familiarity. The three creatures engaged in this mysterious rite were human in shape, and yet human beings with the strangest air about them of some familiar animal" (Wells, ch. 9, 100). The Beast Folk are not frightening because they are monsters. They are frightening because they are almost right (almost human) and that gap between the almost and the actual is where the horror lives.
</p>

<p>
AI-generated music operates in exactly this gap. It has the technical structure of music (melody, harmony, rhythm, lyrical form) but it lacks the interior life that makes music emotionally true. It sounds like it should move the listener, and when it does not, the failure is uncanny rather than simply disappointing. Jonathan Sterne, writing about the history of sound reproduction in <i>The Audible Past</i>, traces how early phonograph developers like Tainter were preoccupied with eliminating what they called the "outside sound" of the machine itself, believing that perfect reproduction required making the apparatus disappear entirely (Sterne 258). Sterne notes that Tainter complained when recordings sounded "hollow, ringing, and musical,” terms that described the grain of the machine rather than the voice (Sterne 258). This anxiety about hollow sound, about reproduction that reveals its own artificiality, anticipates exactly what listeners experience with AI music today. The "hollow" quality Tainter feared is the uncanny gap made audible.
</p>

<p>
What <i>Carole and Tuesday</i> adds to this conversation, and what makes it essential to a critical AI timeline, is its portrayal of a society that has stopped noticing. The people of Mars consume AI music constantly and feel no unease. They have normalized the uncanny to the point where it no longer registers as uncanny at all. Sterne argues that listening itself is a learned, historically situated practice, shaped by the technologies and cultural norms of a given moment. Listeners across history have been trained "to be connoisseurs of the various shades of perfection in tone, thereby learning to distinguish between truth and falsity" in reproduced sound (Sterne 267). In the world of the show, that training has been reversed. The audience has unlearned the ability to hear the difference, or perhaps more likely, has simply stopped caring.
</p>

<p>
Carole and Tuesday themselves are the counterpoint to this normalization. Their music is imperfect and human, made from genuine feeling and real experience, and the show frames it as something radical and rare in their world. The series does not argue that AI cannot make technically competent music, it clearly can, within the fiction and increasingly outside it. What it argues is that technical competence is not the same as authentic expression, and that a world which can no longer tell the difference has lost something important. This is the interpretive claim that earns <i>Carole and Tuesday</i> a place on this timeline. It challenges the narrative of AI as straightforward creative progress by asking what is sacrificed when the uncanny becomes invisible, and when the hollow sound stops bothering anyone at all.
</p>
