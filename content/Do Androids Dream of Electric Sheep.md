
"Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?" is perhaps one of the most widely recognized of the seminal, genre-defining works of Philip K. Dick, not only to the novel’s widespread popularity but also the 1982 blockbuster film adaptation "Blade Runner". Dick questions the very idea of what it means to be human in a dystopian world in an examination of empathy and AI. Although the film focuses on the humanization of the AI powered Androids, or “replicants” as they are referred to in the film as opposed to the “andys” of the novel, the novel ends with a unique subversion, where the protagonist questions his own humanity, after awakening to his own empathy and eventually losing it. Is a human without empathy any different than an unfeeling android? </p> <p>The backbone of the novel is its exploration of humanity through empathy. The religion of the post world war terminus world is called Mercerism, through which people seemingly enter a sort of virtual reality to watch the sisyphean martyr, Mercer, trek up a mountain while being peddled by rocks and boulders, only to fall to the bottom and begin his accent again and again after reaching the top. Through the simulation, people share in a collective consciousness of empathy and empowerment ignited only through these artificial means. Humans in the novel also use a mood organ to artificially generate emotions. While humans are actively removing all human means of emotion and empathy, the androids are subjected to the voight-kampff test, a series of questions which analyze the empathy within the response to see if they are human or not. Is what Dick presents really just a premonition of the smooth-talking AI chatbots we have today, the need to scroll through instagram just to get a hit of dopamine to be able to feel something? Our interactions with technology frighteningly mimic the interactions of humans and technology in the world of Dick’s novel.</p> <p>The protagonist, Deckard, is a cop who is on a mission to retire a series of androids who are living on earth illegally. He spends a large portion of the novel experiencing a series of psychological and hallucinogenic episodes in which he questions his own human identity, even at one point experiencing both an unclear divine intervention from Mercer, and also an episode where he believes he is Mercer. Deckard also slowly accepts his empathy for the androids, which in the end he chooses to ditch, becoming no more human than the very androids he hunted. Deckard represents the fine line between what is human and what is AI. If a human can lose their drive for empathy then what makes us different from an AI who could gain the ability for empathy? Or is that just another hallucination of the AI? </p> <p>Dick’s blend of human and AI behavior suggests a sort of lens to re-examine Mary Shelly’s "Frankenstein". Like Deckard, does Victor's inconsistency, and most often lack, of empathy show him to be as inhuman as the monster? Does the monster’s close resemblance to humans (being made of body parts) make him worthy of our empathy much like the “Andys” of the novel, and possibly more so the “replicants” of the film. Although both the monster and the androids resort of violence, they both desire a place and life in a human world that is denied. Does this denial create the divide between what is human and what is AI, more than the psychological gap? </p> <p>Sources: </p> <p>Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? Philip K.
