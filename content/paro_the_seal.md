# PARO the Seal

PARO the Seal is an advanced, interactive robot developed in Japan by AIST, a leading Japanese industrial automation pioneer. PARO focuses on benefiting elderly patients suffering from dementia and Alzheimer’s with animal therapy that is easily accessible and administrable to hospitals and elderly care facilities where live animals might not be as easily accessible or difficult to use. Made with the intent to assist not just human mental and emotional wellbeing, but also socialization between humans, Japanese developers have created PARO with the idea that technology is a friendly, ideal creation and something that can work alongside humans rather than something that is there to dominate or replace them.
This robotic creation, though used as a means of further developing the assistance of Alzheimer’s and dementia patients, has brought to light the fundamental usage of AI that helps rather than mimic human behavior. The Japanese have considered technology something that is close and friendly, with a society that revolves heavily around technological advancements. While some of our media shows us robots taking over the world, hunting down and killing humans, Japanese media allows them to grow up with a wide variety of media, literature, and toys that help them “[learn] that robots exist to help people be better, be happy” (Maholo, qtd. in Antonelli, 32). This, in turn, creates a society that presents interesting opportunities for promotional items and the development of more AI/technological creations. PARO is made up of five different kinds of sensors: “tactile, light, audition, temperature, and posture sensors, with which it can perceive people and its environment” (PARO Robots U.S., Inc.). The purpose of these sensors is to allow for the recognition of its user, specific behaviors that PARO can react to, as well as the time of day and comfortability of its patient. However, its purpose isn’t to replace an aid or caretaker, but instead eases the stress and emotional deregulation that occurs in patients with Alzheimer’s and dementia to help them interact with other people in a better way. Through repeated interaction with its user (as well as others), PARO “responds as if it is alive, moving its head and legs, making sounds, and showing your preferred behavior” and “imitates the voice of a real baby harp seal” (PARO Robots U.S., Inc.). This is oftentimes much safer than using a living animal due to hospital regulations and the sometimes unpredictable behavior of patients to prevent them and the animal from getting hurt.
My reason for choosing PARO the Seal is because it is quoted as being a “therapeutic robot” (PARO Robots U.S., Inc.), which is similar to how our interactive ELIZA was seen as. Though it had various different fronts for communicating with its user, the one that was labeled as a therapist seemed far from what we would today consider a decent therapist. ELIZA can communicate with us through text, which is arguably simpler than trying to understand the cooes, barks, and movements of an artificially intelligent seal, but it also prompts a sense of aggravation in its inability to develop a conversation correctly. By correctly, I mean in a therapeutic manner: a patient stating that they have an issue, which is acknowledged by ELIZA only by repeating, word for word, what the patient has said in question format, doesn’t necessarily prompt a thorough and healthy conversation about the topic. It mimics human text/speech, providing less room for mental and emotional growth than PARO. PARO creates a feeling of relaxation, easing its patients, while ELIZA (the model used by our class) leads us in circles and never truly ends the conversation at hand.
Both developments of artificial intellectual creations are neither perfect nor bad/negative. There are obviously pros and cons to all that we create, especially because they are replications of ourselves in many ways. However, PARO the seal is a key example of artificially intelligent technology that shows how human development of AI can be for greater good, no matter how small the targeted population. When looking at PARO, it is not something that is feared, but instead something we feel sympathetic and nurturing towards, as though it were a child. This could very well be because it looks like an animal/stuffed animal, but beneath its furry pelt lies the technology that fuels its animations and recognition features.
