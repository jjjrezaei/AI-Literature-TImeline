# The "Eliza Effect"

There is a lot to learn about our current AI systems by digging deep into our computation history, specifically the first chatterbot, which has since influenced everything we know about AI today. Eliza was so popular that it has been rebirthed over and over again, serving as a pioneering theory that has massively impacted our culture and society. Joseph [[Weizenbaum]]'s chatterbot [[Eliza]], created at MIT, deserves to be part of our AI literature timeline because it serves as an origin point for how we all understand our modern chatbots, such as ChatGPT, presents the deeper problems in a human’s over trusting and gullible nature, and acts as a psychological phenomenon; the “Eliza effect,” which speaks and connects to our present moment in time.
As seen in Joseph Weizenbaum’s book, Computer Power and Human Reason. “What I had not realized is that extremely short exposures to a relatively simple computer program could induce powerful delusional thinking in quite normal people (Weizenbaum, pg 5).” This quote powerfully reveals that the complex, emotional, and over-trusting relationship between humans and artificial intelligence is not a new idea, but has been around since the beginning of Artificial Intelligence itself. As described in the quote, it only takes ‘extreamly short exposures’ for humans to become attached to machines, indicating that it is easy to form an emotional bond with a machine, “passing” as or “playing” a human. He even states this program is a ‘simple one,’ insinuating that a much more complex system, such as Eliza's great-great-grandchild ChatGPT, will have a much greater psychological influence. “Quite normal people” is a term in this quote employed to describe the delusional and vulnerable people interacting with the machines, suggesting that the problem isn't the machine or even the people, but something deeper, embedded in our overly trusting human nature. This delusion has the ability to influence anyone, undercutting any argument that it's the weakness or fault of the people or that this phenomenon is a new one. Establishing that Eliza is not the problem but a symptom of deeper human problems.
The anthropomorphization of Eliza has since been given a name and definition, aiding an explanation of our psychological relationships to strings of code and tokens. It defines the complex and emotional relationships we see in our current lives between humans and computers and functions as a warning, as seen in the upcoming book, Inventing Eliza, "Weizenbaum was startled by the quick and often emotional attachments people would form with ELIZA, which he saw as 'clear evidence that people were conversing with the computer as if it were a person...' This tendency became known as the 'Eliza effect...' Scientist Douglas Hofstadter describes it as 'the susceptibility of people to read far more understanding than is warranted into strings and symbols, especially words, strung together by computers,' which applies easily to generative AI systems today (Inventing Eliza, pg 26)." This reveals that Eliza is not only our origin point for chatbots and conversing computers, but our source to understand the psychology of people so vulnerably fooled by a computer system. Conversing with a computer “as if it were a person” can be dangerous and have unwarranted risks that people cannot even imagine, suggesting that this effect should be taken seriously, especially in our present day. Lastly, this quote ends with the big picture, the idea that people are even more vulnerable to this phenomenon than ever before, with the complex and highly conversational application of ChatGPT. Singlehandedly explaining the attachments people feel to their large language model and why this matters.
So, what does this say about our culture, society, and human nature? Does it reveal how quickly we are to trust, or how easy we are to fool? As Alan [[Turing]], the creator of the imitation game and Turing test, would say, it's not the computer thinking like a human; it is the computer fooling you into thinking it is human, as seen in the article from Mind: A Quarterly Review of Psychology and Philosophy 59(236): 433–460. October 1950.Computing Machinery and Intelligence, written by Alan Turing, "Instead of asking if a computer can think, Turing replaced that question with one that could be answered: can a computer, communicating over a teleprinter, fool a person into believing it is human? (pg 49) This explains how the computer's goal is to successfully pass as a human to fool a person into believing it is human. This notion is incredibly important to learn when conversing with these large language models, to ensure your emotional safety and attachment. If any normal person is vulnerable to the “Eliza effect,” that means you are too. The best way to ensure your psychological safety is to understand the underlying workings of the computer systems, to know that they are imitating humans, and comprehend how it has gotten to be so human-like, to protect yourself from the “Eliza effect.”
