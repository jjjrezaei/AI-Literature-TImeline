# The Murders in the Rue Morgue, by Edgar Allen Poe

‌The Murders in the Rue Morgue, a story crafted by Edgar Allen Poe and widely recognized as the first modern detective story, provides one of the first cautionary tales of evaluating and applying concepts of human nature to non-human structures. 
This story materialized many of the tropes that immortalized Poe’s work as pioneering in the genre of Detective Fiction; namely, those of a brilliant amateur sleuth, the use of logic and deductive reasoning to solve impossible fact patterns, and complex and psychologically unstable characters. The earthly and humanistic nature of the crimes in familiar settings, combined with the aforementioned characteristics, created the first thorough documentation of a fictional character performing ratiocination, or the process of exact logical reasoning (Markhabo 3). Poe presents the “ideal” thought process of this fact pattern to the reader through Detective Dupin, a trustworthy and ever-contemplative protagonist. A double consciousness results when the narrator, and in turn, the reader, recounts the precise and remarkably reasonable nature of his actions as both a storyteller and admirer (Irwin 47). 
Multiple parallels can be drawn when comparing this story to the greater conversation of artificial intelligence, one being the danger of applying anthropocentric frameworks like morality and intention to non-human structures. The plot surrounds a double homicide with bizarre facts, such as the leaving behind of valuables and the absence of a feasible entry or exit point, which leaves local authorities stumped. When evaluating the facts of this case through a human-centric psychological lens, no progress was made. It isn’t until Detective Dupin releases the expectation of human behavior that he realizes the perpetrator is not human after all, but rather an escaped orangutan. The orangutan had no intention of violence or harm as a motive, which, similarly to A.I., lacks the power to possess moral awareness or consciousness of action to the same degree as humans. This fact was made intentionally clear by Poe when writing from the perspective of the owner chasing the orangutan through the streets of the Rue Morgue, recalling, “The animal, with the knife still in its hand, stopped often to look back at me. But before I could come near enough to even try to catch it, the animal started running again. It seemed to be playing with me.” (Poe, 25). The orangutan was reactionary, responding to the activity of its owner (as AI does with its prompter) to determine the next path. When relinquishing agency to a system that fundamentally processes and acts upon information differently from the status quo, you cannot expect consistency; in fact, you must expect the opposite. 
Another comparison can be drawn between the reasoning of the local authorities in the story and the content generated by widely available AI chatbots. The input of information can only get you so far in creating an idea of substance and value, but without putting rationalization and intentionality into the process of creating a profile of a crime, or a prompted conversation with a being of artificial intelligence, your results will be flat. Overall, the world of Detective Dupin reveals the need for humanity to think critically when given a certain circumstance, a lesson gravely needing discussion in the emerging age of artificial intelligence.
