# Frankenstein 

Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818) deserves to be included on our artificial intelligence timeline because it reveals that fundamental ethical and philosophical issues related to AI predate the advent of computers. Composed during the early Industrial Revolution, the novel explores the creation of artificial life, the responsibilities of creators, and the social implications of innovation, all issues that remain central in the current world of AI. Incorporating Frankenstein challenges the notion that AI originated solely in twentieth-century computation, and that its foundations have been around long before. In the novel, Victor Frankenstein constructs a being from dead organic matter and animates it through scientific experimentation. However, the critical moment is not the Creature’s animation but Victor’s reaction. Upon witnessing the being open its “dull yellow eye,” Victor recoils in horror and abandons it. This abandonment initiates the ensuing tragedy. Left isolated, the Creature learns language and social behavior by observing the De Lacey family, similar to the way AI observes and replicates its learnings. The Creature teaches himself to read texts such as Paradise Lost and, through this process, develops self-awareness, moral reasoning, and emotional depth. He famously declares, “I ought to be thy Adam; but I am rather the fallen angel,” expressing both his desire for companionship and his understanding of injustice. 
This trajectory closely mirrors modern discussions of machine learning. The Creature is not born violent, as his behavior develops in response to his environment. After repeated rejection, including being attacked when he attempts to approach the De Laceys, he concludes that society has made him a “monster.” His violence emerges from exclusion, not inherent evil. This challenges deterministic narratives that portray artificial beings as naturally dangerous. Shelley instead suggests that created intelligence is shaped relationally, raising urgent questions about responsibility, like “Who is accountable when an artificial being harms others? The creation, or its creator?”
Anne K. Mellor argues in Mary Shelley: Her Life, Her Fiction, Her Monsters (1988) that the novel critiques scientific ambition divorced from ethical responsibility. Victor’s failure is not simply that he creates life, but that he refuses to care for what he has made. This critique resonates strongly with contemporary AI ethics, where debates about oversight, bias, and regulation often center on the obligations of developers and institutions.
Placing Frankenstein on an AI timeline challenges the narrative of linear technological “progress.” Long before the technology we see today, Shelley imagined artificial intelligence as a moral and social problem. The novel shows that AI is not only a technical achievement but also a cultural anxiety about creation, autonomy, and accountability. By beginning our timeline in 1818, we recognize that AI’s history is not just computational, but literary, philosophical, and deeply human.
