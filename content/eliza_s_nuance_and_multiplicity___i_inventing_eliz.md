# ELIZA's Nuance and Multiplicity: <i>Inventing ELIZA</i>

<i>Inventing Eliza</i> is a direct conversation with the author and creator of ELIZA, as well as direct contemplations on many of the issues raised by the creator’s (Joseph [[Weizenbaum]]) book <i>Computer Power and Human Reason.</i> *Inventing [[ELIZA]]*  showcases how we should interact with and think about AI, through interdisciplinary conversation, and consider the invisible history of AI to understand its nuances within our past, present, and future to better assess how we as humans want to move forward with this technology. It is through the close reading of ELIZA as a cultural object, historical object, as the first AI chatbot, and its original code through the voices of professionals in multiple disciplines that this conversation really comes to life. This is the whole point of our class, to bring nuance as well as a broader context (through filling in historical gaps, etc.) to how we interact with and think about AI, and this is exactly the aim of this book especially within its forewards/intro chapter we are privy to—to convince the readers of this mission and create a space where readers can also join in on the conversation (and I’m sure I would include the whole book if it was released)—which is why it deserves a spot on our timeline/within our class


<p style="text-indent: 50px;">In order to continue, it is necessary to understand the emphasis on the interdisciplinary aspect that Weizenbaum calls for, and that the <i>Inventing ELIZA </i> team focuses on. Weizenbaum’s thoughts on science are as follows, “science is creative, that the creative act in science is equivalent to the creative act in art, that creation springs only from autonomous individuals, is such a simple and, one might think, obvious idea” (Weizenbaum 2). And when it comes to language specifically Weizenbaum states, “Belief in the rationality-logicality equation has corroded the prophetic power of language itself” (Weizenbaum 16). Within these statements is a clear cry for both the humanities and sciences to be on equal footing, at the least for both to be in conversation with each other rather than isolated vacuums. And the <i>Inventing ELIZA </i> team values this approach semi-outlined by Weizenbaum, “In this collaborative book, our methods are radically interdisciplinary, developed out of years of conversations among a team with expertise on computer programming, history, literary studies, philosophy, and art” (Berry et al. 31). This means the readers and the world get more context, and a fuller view of ELIZA as well as other topics encompassed in the book. The more perspectives you have, oftentimes means the more nuance you gain on a particular subject/object/etc. This is because you are being presented with a subject from many different angles. The team works and converses together in order to give the readers all that they can.</p>
<p style="text-indent: 50px;">There are two examples within the  <i>Inventing ELIZA </i>  text that will examine the aforementioned components. The first is a nuance that is widely forgotten is the original form of ELIZA and how different it is from our contemporary interactions with AI. In the physical sense ELIZA was huge compared to our everyday modern devices and,</p>
<p style="text-indent: 100px;"> “Weizenbaum’s version of DOCTOR was, as Nick Montfort describes, ‘printed more or less permanently on paper, rather than transiently appearing on screen, [which might] influence how we understand the system as literary and as psychotherapeutic. A session that leaves a printed record, like a diary, may be experienced differently than a transient on-screen encounter’” (Berry et al.; Montfort 19)
<p>The size here is important, because it means that ELIZA could not be moved easily, and also that access was difficult. The permanence and finiteness of paper leaves a physical record that can be easily destroyed while also bringing into the equation of how much space one has for storage/archiving. At the same time, this permanence with the paper is tangible and hard to change. Whereas today’s technology is personal and easily portable. Which adds to Wiezenbaum’s statement, “One would expect man to cathect more intensely to instruments that couple directly to his own intellect, cognitive, and emotive functions than to machines that merely extend the power of his muscles” (Weizenbaum 9). This portability means that these devices go anywhere and everywhere with us causing an even more intense coupling. This extreme reliance is not merely an extension, but acts more as part of our identity (think about phone cases, wallpapers, etc. all showcase who we are). It’s easy to access AI, and honestly you can’t go on the internet without seeing some form of it. New chats can be made quickly, and forgotten just as quickly, but at the same time there is the capacity to store a lot more conversation than just on paper. By including the original physicality of ELIZA, the book brings in extra context that modern readers might not immediately think of which leads to more nuance in understanding the progression of AI. And thus causes us to question: Do we want to deepen this coupling, have it stay where it is, or backtrack? </p>
The second is the inclusion of ELIZA through the lens of gender and identity within the broader scope of computer science’s history, “[[Turing]]’s choice of the initial gender game has ensured that artificial intelligence would remain intertwined with questions of gender and identity…ELIZA picks up where Turing left off, not least with the opening line of dialogue: ‘Men are all alike’” (Berry et al. 26). Furthermore, it is included that none of the women involved in ELIZA were ever named (Berry et al. 27). And an interesting piece uncovered by the team was that the original name of ELIZA was SPEAK (Berry et al. 19). This is particularly intriguing, because it adds missing nuance to how the name changed, as well as the move from a pretty gender-neutral term into a female name. Wiezenbaum gives some insight on why name the object ELIZA, “‘Like G.B. Shaw’s Eliza Doolittle of <i>Pygmalion</i> fame, the program could be taught to ‘speak’ increasingly well, although, also like Miss Doolittle, it was never quite clear whether or not it became smarter’” (Berry et al.; Weizenbaum 27). This is imperative because not only does he attribute the program family to a human character, but also equates smarter with upper-class etiquette (as they tried to teach Eliza within the play to be a passing lady), thus bringing together class and gender issues. (It is interesting that Wiezenbaum gave the machine a human name, and then complained about people applying human qualities to it.) And as sociologist Sherry Turkle exclaims, “‘Very small amounts of interactivity cause us to project our own complexity onto the undeserving object’” (Berry et al.; Turkle 25). So, if a machine equals an object, and the name of the machine is female, then is it not the view that, then, the woman becomes the ‘undeserving object?’ This leads into Judith Butler’s (a Feminist philosopher) claim that, “‘ELIZA both reinforces and challenges assumptions about gendered and embodied communication’” (Berry et al.; Butler 27). There is this push and pull within ELIZA because it is a family of programs rather than one singular recognizable program. The discovery of the original name fills in missing context that creates a more nuanced discussion surrounding ELIZA, and calls into question the lineage left by ELIZA.
<p style="text-indent: 50px;">In the end, these are just two examples and much like the vibe of the book itself, there can be many rabbit holes one could fall into, especially when there are so many lenses to look through. And this is in part due to the nature of ELIZA which the team describes as, “...not stable, but plural, iterative, and endlessly reinventing themselves” (Berry et al.19). But that is exactly why such a nuanced and multifaceted approach is necessary when diving into ELIZA’s history and effect on present technology/AI as well as the future ones too. It is within these multiple view points that nuances spring from, and urge the audience to do the same, to add their own varied perspective to the discussion to gain a fuller understanding on what we as a society are facing. It, thus, changes the way the audience interacts with AI overall, because the nuance causes one to question the often invisible (even deliberately hidden) history of the technology, while then making them think more critically when engaging with it. The next time the audience interacts with AI they will be looking for overarching ideas stated in this work. Questioning how humans should move forward with this information, and the feelings this information creates.</p>
