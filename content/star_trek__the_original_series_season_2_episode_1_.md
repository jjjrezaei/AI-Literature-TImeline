# Star Trek: The Original Series Season 2 Episode 1, "Amok Time"

<p>My first entry on the timeline will be for the show <i>Star Trek: The Original Series </i> (TOS), specifically for season 2, episode 1 titled “Amok Time”. There are a lot of episodes more specifically about AI or computers, but when you consider the show’s main thesis being about human connection, intimacy, and their role in survival (especially on a ship!) I think one of the best episodes for this purpose is “Amok Time”. Between its cultural impact, the themes and world building it reveals, and most importantly its portrayal of the classic dynamic between the central trio of characters (Kirk, Spock, and McCoy), I believe this episode deserves to have an entry on the timeline, in part, to stand for the show and, possibly, the media entity as a whole. I would pretty confidently call this episode the one that has shaped the evolution of the franchise the most. Because of the nuance Star Trek and “Amok Time” takes with its characters, it opens the show to be a metaphor for AI politics as a whole, and also paves the way for future discussions, curations of aesthetics, and characterizations of AI and computer characters.</p>

        
<p>Background info on the episode: </p>
<p>Brief synopsis of the episode from IMDB: “In the throes of his Pon Farr mating period, Spock must return to Vulcan to meet his intended future wife, betrothed from childhood.” </p>

<p>There is no getting around the weirdness of this episode. Spock, a character normally characterized as suppressing his emotions, is essentially in dire need to have sex or he will die, a Vulcan (his race) mating season called “Pon Farr”. He is extremely unhappy and uncomfortable with this, but cannot escape this biological need. When he arrives on his home planet (also called Vulcan) with his best friends Captain Jim Kirk and Dr. “Bones” McCoy, his betrothed wife, T’Pring, refuses to comply, and chooses Jim to battle Spock to the death. At this point, Spock is completely intelligible in the pain of his lust, and tries to kill Jim. After some rolling around in the dirt… very suggestively, McCoy secretly knocks out Jim, making Spock think he has killed him. In the last scene of the episode, Spock learns Jim is alive, and also reveals the need to fornicate has dissipated because he got all his “energy” out fighting Jim. One could assume (and many have) this means Spock was sexually aroused by Jim Kirk, making him gay!</p>

<p>The reception of this episode is a whole other subject on its own. It started the “fuck or die” trope in fanfiction, also known as the “omegaverse”. Not only has this trope spread to nearly every fandom you could think of, its popularity in the <i>Trek</i> fandom at the time (70s and on) drew attention to the show, enough to carry it to still be in the canon of space media today. I could write a lot more, but I’ll instead focus on the argument for this timeline. If you want to know more on the subject, I’d recommend these two videos: </p> <p>https://youtu.be/iCfeD4YANf8?si=lqWlvDrprMED_Sgi Youtuber Strange Aeon’s video on the history of the Omegaverse trope in fanfic</p>
<p>https://youtu.be/4N-YcVclaUI?si=DmWz5IX2Kjr_OdtR Youtuber Jesse Gender’s video on how Trek fanfic saved the show (beware of spoilers in this one)</p>

<p>Other than the prowess this episode holds, I believe this episode displays the main argument of the show very clearly: that no conflict the characters face is more important than a human life, especially the lives of the crew. There is nothing the characters wouldn’t do, especially Jim, McCoy, and Spock, to save each other. They face many aliens, computers, and external forces, but always choose to rebel, sacrifice themselves, or lie in order to save each other. This human-first approach (human meaning all humanoid characters, such as Spock who is half Vulcan) extends to any thinking or feeling being. The dynamic between Jim, Spock and McCoy is a captivating one, full of banter, arguments (esp. Spock and McCoy), and fidelity. Each of the three have a preferred method of how they approach issues, Jim through instinct, Spock through rational, and McCoy through emotion. However, they are not limited to their perspectives, and will often listen, argue, or take on the traits of each other to solve the conflict in the episode. In Antonio Demasio’s book <i>Descartes’ Error: Emotion, Reason, and the Human Brain</i>, he talks about how Descartes’ error is that he separates mind and body. Demasio writes about the importance of co-regulation, saying,</p>
<p>“Knowing about the relevance of feelings in the processes of reason does not suggest that reason is less important than feelings, that it should take a backseat to them or that it should be less cultivated. On the contrary, taking stock of the pervasive role of feelings may give us a chance of enhancing their positive effects and reducing their potential harm. (246)”</p>
<p>In “Amok Time”, it’s conceivable that McCoy and Jim could be disgusted by Spock, or at least off-put by his erratic behavior later in the episode. Instead, they stand by him, even conceivably “taking the fall” for T’Pring to not have to have sex with Spock. The fear of rape in this episode is, interestingly, felt for all parties, as Spock, T’Pring, McCoy, and Jim do not and are not able to consent during the entire episode. However, by their care and efforts, McCoy and Jim are able to twist the situation so that the harm is minimal. T’Pring also regains agency by choosing Jim to fight Spock. If it were not for the balanced and loving relationship the trio had, Spock might not have been saved and the episode would have ended very differently (plenty of fanfiction on this!).</p>
<p>The other major thesis of the show is to be anti-eugenics. From this lens, in “Amok Time”, for instance, the plot revolves around the stopping of a biological phenomenon meant to result in the strongest chance for procreation. By avoiding Pon Farr, Spock is not only going against his culture, but his biology. This argument is often made in this and other episodes through Spock’s struggle with his identity being half human and half Vulcan. Vulcans aren’t supposed to feel emotions, and look down on humans for doing so (this causes racial tensions on both sides). Spock is often at odds with this, and while he usually chooses the Vulcan rational over human emotion, episodes are often resolved when Spock finds a middle ground of thinking. “Amok Time” is no different, with Spock being helped to this middle ground by Jim and McCoy. Having the show question this pushes against eugenics by how it criticizes the importance of biology and genetic engineering. No cultural or biological phenomenon is more important than Spock’s consent or preferences.</p>
<p>This I believe closely acts as a metaphor for AI. Spock is literally unable to act against his “programming” i.e. his culture and biology, but his friends are not bound the same way, and thus can save him. Despite the heavy reliance on futuristic technology in <i>Star Trek</i> in their ship and weapons, it never becomes more important than the safety and happiness of the characters. This value system is also applied to other oppressive forces that may cause characters to act unemotionally or as if they are stuck to a script. Spock’s rationale, for instance, often follows a codelike “if/then” thought pattern. While this metaphor could and has (for many fans of the show) placed Spock’s character in one more closely related to autism or asexuality, I believe that him being often likened to a computer is important, because it reveals how other characters often think of him as operating under one line of thought, rational. Demasio writes about the danger of classification,</p>
<p>“What worries me is the acceptance of the importance of feelings without any effort to understand their complex biological and sociocultural machinery. The best example of this attitude can be found in the attempt to explain bruised feelings or irrational behavior by appealing to surface social causes or the action of neurotransmitters, two explanations that pervade the social discourse as presented in the visual and printed media; and in the attempt to correct personal and social problems with medical and nonmedical drugs. It is precisely this lack of understanding of the nature of feelings and reason (one of the hallmarks of the "culture of complaint"2) that is cause for alarm. (246-247)”</p>
<p>This episode is not resolved because Spock controls his emotions, or because someone falls prey to him, but through the effort of those around him. He is guided, mayhaps similar to the way we train a mindless AI, towards safety, and when he regains his humanity, is able to fully grieve for what he’s done. Interestingly, this is one of the few episodes where we see Spock smile. The nuance this show gives its characters is an argument against the black and white thinking of “AI good or bad” in itself, and indeed paves the way for future episodes of the series and other shows in the same universe (I haven’t even mentioned the character Data yet!) to bring this nuance for those discussions as well.</p>
