# ORIGINS Winter Guard: "The Tower"

<p>ORIGINS World Winter Guard’s 2026 production <i>The Tower</i> represents a striking evolution in the “Sport of the Arts,” merging live performance with digital media to create a layered, multisensory environment. The show’s integration of virtual imagery and embodied choreography transforms the stage into a hybrid space where human movement and technological design co‑produce meaning. In <i>The Tower</i>, ORIGINS demonstrates how digital systems shape perception, agency, and narrative—revealing that AI and machinic mediation are not futuristic novelties but longstanding cultural forces that reorganize how humans experience the world. By staging a performance in which no two vantage points are the same, the production challenges linear narratives of technological progress and reframes AI as a relational, interpretive, and embodied phenomenon.</p>
<p>Winter Guard has historically been a site of experimentation, blending dance, theater, and visual design. In recent years, elite ensembles have increasingly incorporated projection, LED technology, and cinematic staging. ORIGINS’ <i
>The Tower</i> pushes this trajectory further by making digital media not an embellishment but a core structural element of the show’s storytelling. According to the program description, the production “exists not only in movement, but also sensorily and virtually; where choreography and media intersect to form a layered, immersive experience.”(@originscolorguard)</p>
<p>This fusion mirrors contemporary interactions with AI‑driven systems, where physical and digital realities overlap. The Tower—both the literal structure referenced in the show and the metaphorical one created through media—functions as a symbol of mediated perception. Just as AI systems filter, frame, and shape human experience, the show’s digital environment refracts the performers’ actions, creating multiple interpretive pathways for the audience.</p>
<p>A pivotal moment occurs a little over midway through the performance (4:48 in <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vBCeDkPp_jY&list=RDvBCeDkPp_jY&start_radio=1">this video</a>), when the performers move in tight unison while the projected tower begins to distort, refract, and multiply. The choreography emphasizes verticality—reaching, climbing, and collapsing—while the digital tower fractures into shifting geometric planes. This moment creates a visual tension between the stability of the human body and the instability of the mediated environment. The performers’ synchronized movement contrasts with the projection’s fluid, unpredictable transformations. The effect is disorienting: the audience must decide whether to follow the dancers’ embodied narrative or the tower’s digital metamorphosis. This tension dramatizes the central question of human–machine interaction: who shapes perception—the human performer or the technological system framing them? The moment literalizes the idea that meaning emerges through the interplay of bodies and media, not from either alone.</p>
<p>N. Katherine Hayles’ theory of the posthuman provides a powerful framework for understanding <i>The Tower</i>. Hayles argues that in technologically mediated environments, human experience becomes distributed across physical and virtual systems; the body is no longer the sole locus of perception. She writes, “The posthuman subject is an amalgam, a collection of heterogeneous components, a material‑informational entity whose boundaries undergo continuous construction and reconstruction” (Hayles 3). ORIGINS’ production embodies this claim. The performers’ movements are inseparable from the digital architecture surrounding them, creating what Hayles would call a “material‑informational” performance space. The show’s insistence that “no two vantage points are the same” (@originscolorguard) echoes Hayles’ argument that posthuman subjectivity is relational and context‑dependent. Each audience member’s interpretation is shaped by their position, attention, and interaction with the mediated environment—mirroring how individuals engage with AI systems that personalize, filter, and refract information.</p>
<p>Including ORIGINS’ <i>The Tower</i> on our AI timeline expands our understanding of AI beyond algorithms and computation. The production demonstrates that AI’s cultural history includes aesthetic practices that explore mediation, perception, and the fusion of human and technological agency. <i>The Tower</i> challenges the assumption that AI is a recent invention by showing how performance art has long grappled with the same questions that animate AI discourse: How do machines shape human experience? What happens when digital systems become co‑authors of meaning? How does mediation alter embodiment? By staging a world where choreography and digital media are inseparable, ORIGINS reveals that AI is not merely a tool but a cultural condition—one that transforms how stories are told, how bodies move, and how audiences perceive. This makes <i>The Tower</i> a vital addition to any critical, historically informed timeline of AI.</p>
