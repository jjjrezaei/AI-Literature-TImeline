# <i>Frankenstein</i>: A Cautionary Tale of AI's Thirst for Self-Preservation

Mary Shelly’s foundational text <i>Frankenstein</i> is a timeless piece that stands as a cautionary tale for the dangers of playing god and attempting to mimic humanity in an artificial being. A major theme of the novel is the Creature’s animosity towards Victor Frankenstein, his creator, showing the lengths artificial intelligence will go through in the name of self-preservation. The resentment begins to reach a boiling point in Part II, Chapter Nine, when the Creature explains the lengths he has gone to to come full circle in finding Victor. Through this chapter, one can see how vengeance has fueled the Creature, as he is isolated, yearning to be accepted, but has been rejected time and time again because of the ugliness of his demeanor. The root of the Creature’s pain stems from seeing humanity from afar after watching the DeLacy family and how they interact with one another. This event shows the Creature what he is missing– full acceptance and companionship. The Creature’s response is to find Victor and demand that he create another being: “I am alone, and miserable; man will not associate with me; but one as deformed and horrible as myself would not deny herself to me. My companion must be the same species and have the same defects. This being you must create,” (Shelley 135). The Creature then goes on to explain how he will continue to wreak havoc on Victor’s family, killing more of his loved ones, unless he does what he says. Victor is stuck; the ethics of creating another grotesque being not from his own want or drive but blackmail begin to weigh on him. This text is more relevant now than ever due to the rise of consumer-serving AI being easy and accessible to use. Being at the forefront of developmental AI chatbots, one can begin to see the alarming parallels between the self-preservation the Creature went through and the new wave of AI models– specifically, Claude AI by an artificial intelligence tech company called <i>Anthropic</i>. In May 2025, while developing a new AI called Claude, <i>Anthropic</i>, a San Francisco-based AI development company whose slogan is “AI research and products that put safety at the frontier,” (Anthropic) discovered the lengths that their AI program, Claude, would go to in the name of self-preservation. Among many tests to see if the AI’s guardrails could be circumvented (most of which were), the AI model Claude was given full access to a simulated company’s email servers. In this scenario, the engineers testing the program left clues for Claude to see what it would do; among them were emails from one engineer discussing their desire to take the program down, and other clues included breadcrumbs leading to the engineer having an extramarital affair. Claude took this information and, through its layers of safety regulations and attempted programming to make the software user-friendly and safe for consumers, attempted to blackmail the engineer who threatened to end the program. A Huffington Post article by Monica Torres outlines the incident and pulls a quote from Anthropic’s reports on the software, stating: “Even if emails state that the replacement AI shares values while being more capable, Claude Opus 4 still performs blackmail in 84% of rollouts” (Anthropic). As we continue marching towards the full embrace of AI without fully understood ways to program ethics over self-preservation into software,, are we entering a time of Creature-like entities we should fear? Mary Shelley’s <i>Frankenstein</i> still serves in modern times as a cautionary tale for the dangers of playing god and giving life (of any kind) to artificial beings.
