# The Dissolution of NSFNET

April 30, 1995 is the date where the internet’s infrastructure was privatized. NSFNET–the backbone of the internet owned by the U.S. government–was terminated and replaced with private companies (Abbate 183). This was the birth of Internet Service Providers which would take the role of the United States government in the distribution and maintenance of internet infrastructure. This was a big deal–the internet had been created for military purposes and continued to be used as such until 1980’s when it was opened up for university research purposes (Abbate 170). This still did not include the general public and the internet had significant use restrictions. When these restrictions were lifted, there was a massive growth in applications used to socialize. This includes the first online forums (called “USENET” (Abbate 185)) and real-time messaging programs (called BITNET (Abbate 186)). Eventually, this would lend itself to the internet as we know it today. The World Wide Web was created which allowed for web pages and hyperlinks (Abbate 196) and the creation of the first web browsers in 1993 skyrocketed internet usage among the public (Abbate 199). People were able to buy and sell with secure financial transactions and companies began to create their own websites. The dissolution of NSFNET was the fundamental building block towards the world of chatrooms, social media, video games, and more.
The dissolution of NSFNET revolutionized how people perceive computers. Previously, computers connected to the internet were used for military or university research purposes. Computers were a technology which was for calculations, not communication. When the NSFNET (thus U.S. government regulation of the internet) was terminated, the internet could be used for socializing purposes. This changes the relationship between humans and the computer screen–computers could be used as a way to connect with others, to socialize. This potential for relationships through a computer screen legitimized the computer as a form of social interaction, thus legitimizing AI as a passably human intelligence. This may be best understood using Alan [[Turing]]’s “Imitation Game” or what is often referred to today as the “Turing test” which states that an interrogator must be able to discern the man from the computer when in conversation with both. This is intended to replace the question of whether machines are able to think (Turing 3). In order to make the game more fair, Turing decides to make the test such that the interrogator cannot see, touch, or hear the competitors (Turing 4). This, however, puts the human at a disadvantage and contains the pitfalls of [[Cartesian Dualism]] by celebrating the “separation of mind, the ‘thinking thing’ (res cogitanss), from the nonthinking body, that which has extension  and mechanical parts (res exensa)” (Damasio 248). A significant portion of what makes the human distinctly human is removed on the basis of dualistic thinking to be on a flat playing field as the computer. There is no handwriting, voice, or skin that the interrogator could use to prove whether they are talking to a human being or a computer. The end of NSFNET and the social computer programs which followed made this level playing field a reality. The ability to create relationships entirely over the internet means that it becomes possible to socialize without a human signifier (someone’s handwriting, voice, or body) thus legitimizing AI’s existence.
