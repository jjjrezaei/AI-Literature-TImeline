# The Sims



Unlike any other game at the time, <em>The Sims</em> introduced an open world life simulation with no objective. Players would create characters, known as Sims, giving them basic traits. While guiding your character through everyday activities, their personalities, relationships, and habits develop over time through experience. As a result, characters begin to act in ways that feel intentional. Alan [[Turing]] provides a framework for this phenomenon in Computing Machinery and Intelligence, where he proposes a machine that learns through interaction, like a child. <em>The Sims</em> reflects this concept by shaping each character through individualized experience, creating the illusion of autonomy. By modeling intelligence in this way, <em>The Sims</em> helped establish the simulation genre, shifting our relationship with gaming toward systems that prioritize interaction, development, and the experience of life.</p>

<p>Is something as complex as the human mind even possible to replicate through pre-programming? Alan Turing’s response to this problem is simple. “Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child’s?” In <em>Computing Machinery and Intelligence</em> Turing proposes this idea in his attempts to determine whether machines can convincingly imitate human intelligence. Uninterested with the debate of whether machines are capable of thinking, instead he asks if they can make us believe they are. He calls it the “imitation game”. And so, the concept of the “child machine” emerges. He argues that a machine created simply but develops over time through experience and interaction, like a child, is more likely to be perceived as intelligent than a machine that is pre-programmed with complete knowledge from the start. He describes this development as a process of gradual refinement, in which the system improves through trial, error, and adaptation over time. In this way, intelligence is treated as something unique that emerges through learned behavior.</p>

<p><em>The Sims</em> reflects this model of intelligence through its unscripted gameplay, where characters are not fully controlled, and develop traits over time through interaction and experience. Players begin by creating Sims with a limited set of traits, but these traits don’t fully determine how they behave. Sims act on their own, constantly responding to their needs, environment, and the people around them. For example, they get hungry and tired, and develop likes, dislikes, fears and ambitions. Players simply guide their Sims throughout their lives as they age, form relationships, and move through different life stages. Because Sims operate through a needs based system, they often prioritize their own conditions over player input. As a result, they frequently act independently, making decisions without direct instruction and sometimes in completely unexpected ways. Over time, Sims form habits, preferences, and routines that reflect accumulated experience rather than fixed programming. NPC’s alike remember interactions with your character and behave accordingly in future encounters. These developed behaviors give Sims the illusion of autonomy and intelligence.</p>

<p>This perception aligns with Alan Turing’s argument that intelligence is understood through observable behavior rather than internal consciousness. In <em>Computing Machinery and Intelligence</em>, Turing shifts the question away from whether machines truly think and instead focuses on whether their behavior can convincingly imitate that of a human. The Sims are convincing because their imitation goes beyond isolated actions; it unfolds over time, allowing players to observe patterns and changes that resemble human development. The lack of control in the gameplay makes the experience more about observation and engagement. Players watch Sims grow, experience conflict, and build relationships. Because of this observable continuity, players begin to interpret them as individuals with histories and personalities. By modeling Turing’s concept of a child machine, <em>The Sims</em> shifted how players interpret the game itself. The game becomes a form of simulated reality, where players engage with a world that feels alive, not because it is truly intelligent, but because its behavior is convincing enough to be experienced as real.</p>

<p>Alan Turing’s concept of the child machine argues intelligence is something that develops through experience, and cannot be pre-programmed. <em>The Sims</em> actualizes this idea by shaping characters through individualized experiences, producing behaviors that create the illusion of autonomy. In doing so, the game demonstrates that intelligence is not something that can be proven internally; intelligence is perceived through interaction. The popularization of <em>The Sims</em> and the life simulation genre serves as evidence that Turing’s “imitation game” functions in practice. Ultimately, <em>The Sims</em> earns its place on the AI timeline not because it creates true intelligence, but because it reveals that the perception of intelligence is enough to reshape how we understand and engage with the digital world.</p>
