In 2011, a little language app called Duolingo was developed. With over 37 million users and a company now worth over 7 billion dollars, this app, which allows users to learn a new language interactively and rewards consistent use, has recently integrated AI into its multifunctional platform, providing an individualized experience for every user. All this wouldn’t be possible without the inventions and interrogation of Joseph [[Weizenbaum]]’s [[ELIZA]],  the first artificial intelligence chatbot program ever written. What made ELIZA so successful besides its unseen advancement was its ability to “capturing the creative energy, playfulness, and iterative design that made ELIZA so unexpectedly lifelike.” (Inventing ELIZA, Janet H. Murray) This same formula is credited with Duolingo's success and its ability to keep its interactive human counterparts coming back for more.

What makes ELIZA and Duolingo so similar is their user functionality and their ability to interact like humans. (imitation game, [[Turing]] test) Janet H. Murray says, “it’s not a benchmark for machine intelligence, but really a measure for machine gullibility,” and this is how Duolingo thrives. Duolingo uses artificial intelligence to meticulously captivate and monitor its users, wanting them to come back for more. This success is in part due to Duolingo’s notification system, which, like ELIZA, is “instrumental, technical, and calculating, not just about mathematics or computing, but about everything, including human life, feelings, and communities.” Duolingo uses an animated, cute green owl to notify users when it’s time to come back and resume their language skills, but it does it in a way that is able to relate to the user once it has accumulated knowledge of the said user using it, allowing the cute little green owl to be more persuasive. The app icon itself, the green owl mascot, slowly morphs into a sad, not-so-cute owl, tugging at the user’s heartstrings and allowing a sort of guilt trip to set in. One can refer back to the first ELIZA conversation and see how effective this can be. “ELIZA has set a template for human-computer conversations,” and Duolingo is the definition of a language app that is in full imitation mode. 

Duolingo recently introduced AI at the Max Tier, which allows paid subscribers to have a conversation with AI in their newly learned language, assisting and teaching users with pronunciation, spelling, and role-playing. ELIZA “established the conditions and limits under which natural- language communication between man and machine could be made plausible.” Duolingo does this and more.  The success in this comes from a sense of non-judgment from the AI, allowing users to feel more comfortable and not be obligated to do something on someone else’s time, but rather when they like. (depending on whether a guilt trip notification was received.) And just like the reasoning behind the name of ELIZA, Duolingo also uses this same tactic. While ELIZA was given the name “to emphasize that it may be incrementally improved by its users, since its language abilities may be continually improved by a ‘teacher,’ ” Duolingo’s name comes from the connection between the process and learning of two languages, allowing it to be accessible for everyone. The word lingo, in fact, is a newly coined slang term, which in turn shows the advancement and understanding of the foundation ELIZA has given Duolingo, making it more accessible to everyone. 

“The tendency to attribute empathy and invest private feelings into a computer puzzled Weizenbaum.” Often referred to as the “ELIZA effect,” this exact term is the model and basis for Duolingo's success and that of its returning users. Humans are creatures of habit and feeling, and thanks to the ELIZA effect, Duolingo and many other AI platforms have Weizenbaum to thank; even Weizenbaum himself may feel some sort of guilt trip about his invention.
