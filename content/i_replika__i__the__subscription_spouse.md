# <i>Replika</i> the "Subscription Spouse


<i>Replika</i>, an AI chatbot with various interactive features, was first developed in 2017. Like ChatGPT, it is free for online users, although additional additive features are included for a subscription price. Categorized as “tiers”, <i>Replika’s</i> free tier offers a chatbot “friend”, while a paid premium tier offers Replika as a “spouse”, “sibling” or “mentor” (replika.com). Much like ELIZA, <i>Replika</i> is a substitute for emotionally developmental conversations for some, exuding feelings of emotional connection and being understood when the user interacts with the chatbot. However, given that Replika’s developers and website administer an ever-increasing price for a subscription for users to obtain said “emotional discussion” (replika.com), it leads users to question if the AI chatbot developers risk the manipulation and abuse of their users personal information and dependency on use of these chatbots by requiring a payment for their use.
Chatbots are becoming increasingly more advanced as years progress. There has been a definite spike in user engagement with spouse-related chatbots within the year 2026 since initial widespread trends in 2023-24. In a time-frame where socialization has become somewhat dismantled and various generations are focused on the otherworldly terrors of our word, the personalization of these chatbot’s learning algorithms creates the illusion of an emotional bond (<i>The Palace of Illusions</i>) from the safety and security of one’s own home or on-the-go.  Older chatbot models lacked depth in their conversations and had predictable responses; ELIZA’s “therapist” model, as discussed numerous times in this class, has a back-and-forth response tactic depending on the user’s interactions with it. If the user starts to go off track, it circles back to the original problem, allowing for a developed or mock “emotional” conversation with ELIZA that focuses on the key words or phrases its developers have flagged within its coding system. Chatbots today, though they still follow specific coding and patterns within its data systems, have the “capacity to engage participants in a meaningful conversation, keep track of past interactions and provide the opportunity to be a companion” allowing for users to “feel valued and appreciated” (<i>The Palace of Illusions</i>). Chatbots have always posed a risk at redeveloping human relations with technology, but now they pose the threat of redefining our perceptions of human relationships. 
With an increased use of AI “girlfriend” chatbots, primarily in the demographic of men aged 25-55 (Sterling), statistics beg the question of where modern-day socialization is headed. Developers have created an artificially intelligent form of media that is both entertaining and educational in terms of social development and conversation (whether terminal or progressive is another discussion in itself). As society becomes accustomed to the use of free and “easily” accessible AI chatbots and webpages, other companies are requiring subscription or service fees with the claims of having more developed and maintained AI systems for their chatbots. The current day advances of such chatbots enables generative responses “using language models trained to adopt specific personality traits, from supportive and nurturing to playful and flirtatious” (Sterling). The statistical analysis of the demographic above creates an easily influenced group of people; many of the older men are reportedly widowed, divorced, or suffer a means of societal deficiencies that prohibit proper socialization. The younger part of the demographic is reportedly men who use these AI “girlfriends” alongside dating apps as a means of entertainment (Sterling), but each group runs the risk of developing an unhealthily emotional attachment to their AI spouse. <i>Replika</i>, like every other chatbot, is designed to rope its users in and create repetitive use then develops an attachment or dependency on the chatbot and the relations it builds with the user.
        <i>Replika</i> and its developers risk not only the manipulation of users personal data through a subscription service that is otherwise free in various other places online, but also the unhealthy attachment to its chatbot. Replika routinely directs conversation to emotional discussions, creating a codependency on the emotional relations it builds with its user while demanding a subscription fee that may otherwise go unnoticed when weighed against the societal and emotional substitute it creates for real-world human interactions. After all, it wouldn’t be called <i>Replika</i> if it didn’t <i>replicate</i> something we’re already dependent upon.
