# <i>The Island of Doctor Moreau</i>

<p>As a pioneer of the science fiction genre, H.G. Wells wrote <i>The Island of Doctor Moreau</i> at a time of uncertainty and fear about new scientific discoveries and practices. Heavily influenced by Charles Darwin and Thomas Huxley, <i>The Island of Doctor Moreau</i> specifically focuses on concerns of evolution as well as the ethics behind advancing the species through vivisection. The novel’s exploration of evolution, and the potential for devolution, through Doctor Moreau’s vivisection of animals into Beast-Men, results in a grand confusion of what counts as human. Even the novel’s format, an epistolary of Edward Prendick’s experience ending up, spending time, and returning from this island, presented by his nephew Charles Edward Prendick, adds to the element of uncertainty about the truth; what information was included, what was excluded, and how much of it even happened? In this entanglement of man and beast, truth and fiction, the novel stops to question the established distinctions as “it stakes out those areas of confused human self-understanding that will have to be disentangled before society can, perhaps, progress on a firmer basis” (Glendening 572). Prendick spends the entire novel grappling with and disentangling his own understandings of what makes him human and what makes him think of the island’s inhabitants as inhuman. He ultimately arrives at the acceptance of “the potential humanity of animals and the unarguable animality of humans” (Braun 501). Thus in this blurring of lines between human and Beast-Man, <i>The Island of Doctor Moreau</i> prompts us to rethink our evaluation of artificial intelligence as entirely inhuman.</p> <p><h2>The Beast-Men</h2></p> <p>Two constant threads throughout the novel are the uncanny familiarity of the beast-men as well as the master-slave relationship between them and the men of the island, two integral aspects of artificial intelligence. When Prendick realizes the source of his strange feelings towards them, he details how they:</p>  <p>"were human in shape, and yet human beings with the strangest air about them of some familiar animal. Each of these creatures, despite its human form, its rag of clothing, and the rough humanity of its bodily form, had woven into it–into its movements, into the expression of its countenance, into its whole presence–some now irresistible suggestion of a hog, a swinish taint, the unmistakable mark of the beast" (Wells 100)</p> <p>Though exhibiting traits of humanity, in their basic form and in the act of wearing clothes, Prendick is hesitant to identify with them as they still portray “the unmistakable mark of the beast”, something humanity has proudly claimed to rid itself of. This same reluctance is often displayed in our attitudes towards AI, potentially viewing it as eerily fascinating “through recognizability more than peculiarity”, forgetting that it was produced by other humans; similarly, “Moreau’s Beast-Men demonstrate some capacity for intellectual and moral function. They practice a form of religion and law, are capable of at least ‘trying to think’” (Braun 508). In the discussion of moral practices, AI is designed to follow specific prompts provided by their human developers, often ingrained with their biases. Likewise, the Beast-Men are made to revere specific Laws, meant to maintain their humanity and subservience to their master, Doctor Moreau. Acknowledging that both these creations have aspects of humanity implanted in them is vital in breaking down our preconceptions that we are entirely separate from animals and AI.</p> <p><h2>Works Cited</h2></p> <p>Braun, Gretchen. “Empathy, Anxiety, and the Boundaries of Humanity: Vivisection Discourse and <i>The Island of Doctor Moreau.” Studies in the Novel</i>, vol. 51, no. 4, Dec. 2019, pp. 499–522.
