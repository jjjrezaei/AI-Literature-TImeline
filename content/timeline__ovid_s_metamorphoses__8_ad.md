# Timeline: Ovid’s Metamorphoses (8 AD)

Timeline: Ovid’s Metamorphoses (8 AD)

In book 10 of Ovid’s Metamorphosis, we follow the story of Pygmalion and his sculpture named Galatea. It is important to note that Ovid wasn’t the originator of Pygmalion and his story, but he did popularize it in a literary form, which was then followed by numerous plays and eventually Emily Short’s Galatea (2000). Furthermore, the statue’s name, Galatea, was added in later editions after Ovid’s Metamorphoses. Throughout this story, Pygmalion, disgusted by women and their pitfalls, attempts to make the perfect woman. As time moves on, Pygmalion falls in love with his sculpture. Lamenting his circumstances, he prays to the gods, kisses, adores, and dresses his sculpture. Eventually, the gods grant his wish of imbuing the statue with life. This Greek myth laid the groundwork for countless aspects of machines and artificial intelligence. The most prominent tie of Pygmalion to artificial intelligence is the act of humanization and anthropomorphizing of the inanimate. Additionally, Pygmalion can be seen as a precursor (as many Greek myths are) to the pattern of using women to be the voice of machines.  In his story, Pygmalion desires for his creation to become alive; this created life is artificial. As we see in Joseph Wiezenbaum’s ELIZA, humans have trouble viewing the artificial as unliving and unfeeling. In Wiezenbaum’s Power and Human Reason, he writes about his very own secretary creating and maintaining an eerie relationship with ELIZA. In these stories, we see a common theme of projecting human characteristics onto the inanimate (Galatea, ELIZA, ChatGPT, etc.). 
Another major characteristic of artificial intelligence that stems from Greek Myths is the subjugation of women. Along with subjugation and discrimination for women, typical manifestations include women in roles of servitude and limited use of language. These women are only allowed to talk within limits and rules. Using Emily Short’s Galatea to build on Pygmalion, we see that he hated women, and he wanted control. These facts help us to find these myths as origins of machine learning and the pattern of women being used as servants and discarded when men are done with them. Ovid writes, 
“Pygmalion loathing their lascivious life,
Abhorr'd all womankind, but most a wife…” (BK X). 
In Galatea, the statue speaks of how, after her animation, Pygmalion didn’t want her. For instance, “Finally I made him talk to me, and he told me that he didn't want me to be alive.  He hadn't made me to be alive.  He asked me, in fact, if I would go back to being a statue." (Short 2000). In Short’s version of Pygmalion, we see his deep frustration and abhorrence of women permeate his interactions with his ‘perfect woman.’ Even though it was his initial wish, Pygmalion doesn’t want to talk to Galatea. Part of this is due to the fact that he cannot control her speech. His hatred and frustration come back the moment Galatea gains a semblance of sentience. Following this lineage of the Greek hatred of women, we see the implementation of female voices in many virtual assistants. A way to keep women serving and catering to men. ELIZA is based on a female character from a later edition of Pygmalion’s myth. ELIZA’s name is inspired by the program's learned ability to ‘speak’, as in George Bernard Shaw’s play. Apple’s Siri and Amazon’s Alexa use a female voice to respond to commands. Even more so, Galatea proves herself to be as artificial as artificial intelligence. While LLMs and generative AI scrape language, Galatea learned from people. In the game, we come across a housekeeper who drops off food. Additionally, Pygmalion invites buyers to view her. All these interactions informed her of what human life looks and acts like. For example, “You become aware of her breathing -- the slight expansion of her ribs, the soft exhalation -- natural, and yet somehow studied.” (Short 2000). Not quite human, but informed enough to be convincing. “Somehow studied” leads us to believe that her habits are just another mimic that we see with machines trying to pass the [[Turing]] Test. Similarly, ChatGPT learns from prior conversations with its users.

Pygmalion and his desire to instill life in creation are mirrored in countless works that come after. Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein, H.G. Wells’ The Island of Dr. Moreau, and users of ELIZA. These texts are all frameworks we use to look at the longer timeline of Artificial Intelligence. They help us to see A.I. as something with a history and not as an entirely new concept. Emily Short’s perspective on Pygmalion and Galatea further plays into the feminist theory that once instilled with life, the life becomes forgotten. For instance, The Beast Folk, Frankenstein’s Creature, and Galatea through her own interactive fiction game (2000). In each of these instances, we are faced with the misogynist ideals of servitude, often portrayed as others or, more importantly, women.
