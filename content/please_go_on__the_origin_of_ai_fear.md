# Please Go On: The Origin of AI Fear 

Many people today believe that ChatGPT is synonymous with the birth of AI. In reality, it is but one iteration of a language learning mode–not at all the first of its kind, but merely the most famous. The true first chatbot was created by Dr. Joseph [[Weizenbaum]] in the basement of an MIT lab, later named ELIZA, after George Bernard Shaw's character in his play Pygmalion. Weizenbaum was shocked and concerned by the public's immediate devotion to a machine whose ability to mimic conversation through the traversal of characters and source code was almost unequivocally mistaken for an understanding of human concepts like language, compassion, and curiosity–even by colleagues whom Weizenbaum described as "...trust[ing] only modern science to deliver reliable knowledge of the world" (Weizenbaum 10). This discovery led Weizenbaum to shut down ELIZA, spending the rest of his career warning against blind faith in the humanity of a machine that can only mimic an interpretation of the concept. Rather than focusing on AI as a recent innovation, closely reading the introduction to <i>Inventing ELIZA</i> (a collaborative scholarly volume presenting the rediscovered original source code of ELIZA) alongside Joseph Weizenbaum’s <i>Computer Power and Human Reason</i> to examine how early responses to ELIZA reveal that contemporary anxieties about AI are not new, but foundational. These texts demonstrate that ELIZA did not just anticipate modern AI systems, it sparked the first substantial fears about the dangers of their use.  

ELIZA is programmed to prompt the user, famously with "Please go on." This simple phrase is evident of the logic of these systems–in order for ELIZA to produce coherent responses with its user, it must reproduce the very words and phrases from the user input, thus, the user must go on. Weizenbaum describes how this truth fell short, as even a highly-educated audience endowed "a technology it does not understand" with "enormously exaggerated attributions" (Wezeinbaum 7), a critique that remains relevant in the age of ChatGPT and its growing cultural authority, and showcases the necessity for tracing the origins of the questions about consequences of AI chatbot use we are still asking today. 

He further notes that discussions surrounding AI involve “coping” (Weizenbaum 8), the implication of which reflects a conscious effort taken to mitigate a present anxiety or fear. His nonfiction novel, published 10 years later in 1976, explains that conversations concerning "the impact of computer on man and his society" have "intensified in the last few years" (Weizenbaum 11), suggesting an increase in fear following the ELIZA release was a spark to discussions about AI dangers. This claim is echoed by the authors of <i>Inventing ELIZA</i>, stating unambiguously that ELIZA "spawned delusional experiences of animation and also the first coherent arguments against the dangers of AI" (Ciston et al. 11). The projection of life and intention onto a machine remains central to how users interact with AI systems today, and is the root of much of our modern awe and fear. 

Taken together, these authors present analysis and Weizenbaum's relevant critique show that fear surrounding artificial intelligence is not the product of modern day advancements and systems like ChatGPT, but is rooted in the earliest encounters with ELIZA. It is clear that the questions we ask about AI chatbots today–about agency and trust–emerged at the very moment of its inception, and that we have not yet reckoned with Weizenbaum's most pressing fears. 

Ciston, Sarah, et al. Inventing ELIZA: How the First Chatbot Shaped the Future of AI. MIT Press, 2023. 

Weizenbaum, Joseph. Computer Power and Human Reason: From Judgment to Calculation. W. H. Freeman, 1976.
