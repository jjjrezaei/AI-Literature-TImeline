# Karen From SpongeBob Squarepants 

A universe that follows a fry cook sponge through his life experiences and relationships in the bustling underwater city of Bikini Bottom, SpongeBob SquarePants is an animated show that introduced a generation to the power of an optimistic outlook. Beyond being a kids' television show that’s run consecutively on Nickelodeon since 1999, the reality created by Stephen Hillenburg profoundly imitates tropes and rhetoric often experienced in the pursuit of the American Dream, mainly through tropes and common character archetypes (Tarr, 9). SpongeBob is the optimistic protagonist, Patrick is the flawed yet loyal friend, Mr. Krabs is the money-hungry superior, but an interesting interpretation is taken with Karen, a figure of artificial intelligence. Her character is the witty and reluctant spouse to the antagonist, a manifestation of how an AI being can be an independent and rational thinker in complex situations. This, presented in a world that depicts human reality, extends the capability of an AI being beyond those determined by pioneers of the subject like [[Weizenbaum]] and [[Licklider]], making the show a speculative model of possible AI inclusion in the future.
Physically, Karen is a computer that receives different human attributes as the complexity of the show increases. She starts as a motherboard fixed to a wall, only made mobile after being transfigured to the top of a moving cart. This elongated and free-moving posture, along with the addition of permanent arms and gloved hands in season nine, creates a human-like appearance that enables expression. Despite her physical form changing throughout the show, her existence as a free-thinking character is made apparent from the first season, where in episode three, “Plakton!”, she helps her husband attempt to steal the coveted Krabby Patty formula. She does not act as a tool for Plankton to use throughout the episode, but rather gives sarcastic remarks and dry judgment to the plan of entering SpongeBob’s brain. This input influences direct changes in the plan. Karen is not recognized as being owned or controlled throughout this process, but rather a partner and equal peer that ultimately disapproves of the end procedure. She is not berated or beaten by her husband in a power dynamic of machine and man (or plankton), maintaining a vein yet personable dialogue that is generally unfamiliar when talking about beings of artificial intelligence.
The physical attributes of Karen, as well as her position as a wife and contributor within her society, contradict the ideas presented in key works of literature on Artificial Intelligence. In Computational Power and Human Reasoning, Joseph Weizenbaum argues “that a line dividing human and machine intelligence must be drawn… I would argue that, however intelligent machines may be made to be, there are some acts of thought that ought to be attempted only by humans.” (Weizenbaum, 9, 13). He frames this claim by arguing that computers cannot simulate human reasoning. Karen, while being a constructed machine, presents logic that is not a computational output, but set in personality and independent evaluation. Even though J.C.R. Licklider acknowledges a time may exist where anthropomorphic intelligence is surpassed by machine, he projects his ideal man-computer symbiosis in which the machine aids and acts as an extension of the man. Karen disrupts this hierarchy, frequently positioning herself throughout the series as the more rational and consistently critical member of the relationship. Her stubborn resistance acts as an indicator that her intelligence is not a characteristic of human interaction, as Licklider theorizes; that is just her personality.
