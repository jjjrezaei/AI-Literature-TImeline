# Paradise Lost

<p>John Milton’s <i>Paradise Lost</i> is one of the earliest literary explorations of artificial intelligence, free will, and the responsibility of the creator. In this world, both angels and humans are endowed with autonomy, capable of choosing obedience or rebellion. In this epic, readers get to observe how these beings interact with their creator. Milton most notably presents two contrasting models of the relationship between creator and creation through the characters Adam and Satan. While Adam represents a creation that is nurtured and guided by its creator, Satan embodies a being cast out and forced into rebellion. Mary Shelley draws on these models in <i>Frankenstein</i>, where the creature recognizes that although he was created like Adam, his abandonment by Victor Frankenstein makes Satan the more accurate reflection of his condition. Exploring these relationships with God raises an important ethical question: what responsibilities does a creator have toward a being capable of independent thought and choice?</p>

<p>Milton’s epic begins in the aftermath of Satan’s failed rebellion against God. After rebelling against his creator, Satan and the other fallen angels are defeated and eternally cast out of Heaven into Hell. Satan eventually makes his way to Earth and enters the Garden of Eden, a place God created for his new human creations. There he encounters Adam, the first human created by God, and Eve, a companion created specifically for him. God instructs the humans not to eat from the Tree of Knowledge, warning that if they do, they will die. Taking the form of a serpent, Satan tempts Eve with the promise of wisdom. After she eats the forbidden fruit, Eve persuades Adam to do the same. They are expelled from Eden as punishment, but they are not abandoned.</p>

<p>Adam represents Milton’s clearest example of a creation that is nurtured and guided by its creator. After being formed by God and placed in the Garden of Eden, Adam is not left to navigate existence alone. God communicates directly with him, explaining the structure of the world and the responsibilities that accompany his existence. Adam is given dominion over Eden, companionship in Eve and the angels, boundaries, ideas of morality, and most importantly the command not to eat from the Tree of Knowledge. Milton presents a relationship of ongoing responsibility between creator and creation. Even after the Fall, Adam is not abandoned by his creator. Instead, he is shown a vision of humanity’s future redemption, as the angel explains that <i>“One greater Man / Shall come… to restore us, and regain the blissful seat.”</i> (<i>Paradise Lost</i>, Book XII). Even in punishment, Adam continues to receive guidance and the promise of eventual restoration.</p>

<p>While Adam represents a creation that continues to receive guidance from his creator, Satan’s relationship with his creator is defined by rebellion and permanent abandonment. Like Adam, Satan was created by God and given intelligence and autonomy. However, after rebelling, he is permanently exiled from Heaven along with the other fallen angels. Unlike Adam, whose disobedience is later met with the promise of eventual redemption, Satan receives no possibility of reconciliation with his creator. As God explains, <i>“Man therefore shall find grace; / The other none.”</i> (<i>Paradise Lost</i>, Book III). Satan therefore represents a form of created intelligence permanently separated from the care and guidance of its creator. His identity becomes defined by opposition to the authority that created him, leading him to declare that <i>“Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heaven.”</i> (<i>Paradise Lost</i>, Book I). Through this contrast, Milton presents two very different outcomes for created beings who exercise autonomy: one in which the creator continues to guide and redeem the creation, and another in which separation from the creator becomes permanent.</p>

<p>Mary Shelley directly references Milton’s epic when the creature in <i>Frankenstein</i> reads <i>Paradise Lost</i>. While attempting to understand his own existence, he interprets his relationship with his creator by comparing himself to Milton’s characters. At first, he identifies with Adam, explaining that <i>“Like Adam I was created, apparently united by no link to any other being in existence.”</i> However, he recognizes a crucial difference. While Adam was created within a relationship of guidance and care, the creature himself was abandoned with no instruction, companionship, or protection in this strange new world. Because of this isolation, the creature states, <i>“Many times I considered Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition.”</i> Through his analysis of the text, the creature is forced to question the ethical nature of his relationship—or lack thereof—with his creator.</p>

<p>Through the characters Adam and Satan, <i>Paradise Lost</i> presents two contrasting models of the relationship between creator and creation. Adam represents a relationship defined by care, guidance, and the promise of redemption, while Satan illustrates a relationship permanently separated from that care and compassion. Mary Shelley draws on these models in <i>Frankenstein</i>, where the creature reflects on his own creation and feelings of isolation, recognizing that his suffering did not have to unfold the way that it did. By invoking Milton’s epic, Shelley highlights the ethical consequences of creation without responsibility. <i>Paradise Lost</i> therefore continues to shape discussions about what obligations a creator has to guide and care for the life they bring into existence.</p>
