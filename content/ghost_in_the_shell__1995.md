# Ghost In The Shell (1995)

<P>Mamoru Oshii’s 1995 movie <i>Ghost In The Shell</i> is a thought-provoking tale, one that dangles audiences within its cyberpunk tech noir setting. It is revealed within the movie that artificial intelligences are capable of mimicking “Ghosts,” human minds within mechanical cyberbrains.This essentially brings up the question of what mental distinctions remain unique to human beings if these intelligences are capable of rendering human traits and potential obsolete. Is cybernetic advancement worth sacrificing individuality within societies? </P>

<P>Taking place within the future of 2029, cybernetic technology has advanced to the point where humans are more often than not, augmented. The creation of the cyberbrain (a human brain wrapped in cybernetics) leads to the term “Ghost,” referring to the consciousness inhabiting a cybernetic body (the “Shell.”) Audiences follow Major Motoko Kusanagi, a team leader for Public Security Section 9, a security and intelligence department. The Major is the main example of someone who has augmented her entire body, remaining as a cyberbrain in a metal shell due to an illness from youth. She is sent to investigate a rogue “Ghost” who has since become sentient within the “Shell” it inhabits after wandering networks and the internet, known as the Puppet Master. The Puppet Master eventually comes around to explain its purposes in wanting political asylum to Kusanagi. It describes its experiences travelling through the Net, coming to the conclusion that the makeup of life is to reproduce and die. Essentially, the AI wants to exist in a physical brain that will eventually pass. Kusanagi throughout the movie struggles with her own humanity, and as such, agrees to Puppet Master’s proposition to merge their “Ghosts” together.</P>

A theme that remains prevalent throughout the duration of the movie is the concept of [[Cartesian dualism]]. As proposed by Descartes, the body and mind are separate entities that interact with one another. British philosopher Gilbert Ryle’s critique of this hails from his book The Concept of Mind, where he argues that the mind is a philosophical illusion. He states that “When someone is described as knowing, believing or guessing something, as hoping, dreading, intending or shirking something, as designing this or being amused at that, these verbs are supposed to denote the occurrence of specific modifications in his (to us) occult stream of consciousness.” (11) Within the spheres of AI discourse alongside <i>Ghost In The Shell</i>, the movie dances along the edge between biological consciousness and machine intelligence. While Kusanagi seemingly loses her individual “mind” after merging with the Puppet Master, there is an argument to be made as Ryle disputes Descartes’ notion of the mind being private. In this, Ryle claims that behavioral dispositions are observable by others. The people with cybernetic brains and minds that traverse and browse the Internet become susceptible to being hacked, their privacy is not guaranteed. Puppet Master is capable of seeing these minds, forming its own sentience in wanting to inherently be human, or have agency like one. AI might not be at the level of making demands for bodies or dreaming of worlds that allow them to be human, but do humans wish or need to advance to the point where AI and human become indistinguishable? Does a human need both a mind and a body? Are they truly separate? AI is predominantly starting to shape technological advancements, so the question remains of what will stay unique to humans, perhaps even other sentient beings.
