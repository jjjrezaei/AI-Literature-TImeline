
Published in 2010, _Apocalyptic AI_ is an interdisciplinary study that bridges religious studies, science and technology studies (STS), media studies, and AI history. Robert Geraci argues that contemporary visions of artificial intelligence, robotics, and virtual reality are deeply shaped by religious narratives of transcendence, salvation, and apocalypse. Rather than treating AI purely as a technical field, Geraci further demonstrates that many AI researchers, futurists, and technologists draw, often unconsciously, on theological ideas about salvation, immortality, and the destiny of humanity. A central argument of the book is that AI discourse functions as a form of modern myth-making. Geraci analyzes influential technologists and futurists whose work envisions uploading human consciousness, achieving digital immortality, or creating godlike artificial minds. These visions closely parallel Christian and other religious eschatologies: resurrection, heaven, salvation, and the end of human history, themes we’ve addressed in our course readings. 
We can apply Descarte’s mind-body problem here: Geraci argues that modern AI dreams (especially mind uploading and digital immortality) are essentially Cartesian fantasies made technological. If the mind is separable from the body, then it can theoretically be transferred to a machine. Here we revisit Damasio, who famously argues that reason cannot be separated from emotion and embodiment, and we have our perennial critique of the dream of disembodied intelligence as it persists in AI culture. We can also connect Geraci to Gilbert Ryle and his critique of the “ghost in the machine.” Geraci demonstrates that AI discourse continues to reproduce this “ghost” by imagining the mind as uploadable software (transhumanisms, the Singularity?). Agreeing with the general thesis of our course, Geraci shows that AI is not a sudden invention but the continuation of a philosophical tradition, the “beginning” of which we seek to record.
_Apocalyptic AI_ deserves a place on our timeline because it marks a key milestone in documenting the philosophical-religious relations in artificial intelligence narratives, and the continued recognition that AI is not just a technological field but a cultural and spiritual phenomenon. Geraci argues that AI worldviews both inform and are informed by culture, discussing "cyber-theology" in relation to other apocalyptic theological traditions. By highlighting these hidden and not-so-hidden religious narratives embedded in AI discourse, we might understand more fully why AI matters so deeply to society and why debates about these technologies often feel existential. Including this entry keeps in line with our goal to create a record of technological progress, a history of human imagination, belief, and the search for transcendence through machines.
