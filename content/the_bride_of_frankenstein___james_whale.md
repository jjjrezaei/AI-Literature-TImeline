# The Bride of Frankenstein - James Whale

The first timeline entry I want to add to our AI timeline is the 1935 film The Bride of Frankenstein, directed by James Whale. The original sequel to the 1931 film Frankenstein, it is widely regarded as one of the greatest horror films ever made and remains a landmark in science fiction history.  Since we read Frankenstein by Mary Shelley in class, I figured this would be the best way to connect class information to something I personally felt a connection with, and that was The Bride of Frankenstein. This film offers a bridge between nineteenth-century anxieties about creation and twentieth-century cinematic imaginings of artificial life. Personally, I have always found The Bride of Frankenstein more interesting than the original film. It deepens the emotional and psychological dimensions of the Creature, making his longing, loneliness, and desire for companionship even more central: it further expands on the human-ness of the monster.

In the original novel, Frankenstein, the Creature brings up his desire for a companion by telling Victor, “You must create a female for me with whom I can live in the interchange of those sympathies necessary for my being. This you alone can do; and I demand it of you as a right which you must not refuse to concede.” His desire for a partner frames companionship as a “right,” but it simultaneously reveals a troubling fact: he speaks for a being who does not yet exist. He assumes her desires, her consent, and her purpose before she has any voice at all. This strain of longing becomes even more apparent within Whale's 1935 adaptation, when the Bride is brought to life only in the final act. Visually, she is iconic. Her stylish hairdo and electric makeup remain a vision in pop culture to this day. But, despite her iconic status, she has no name, almost no dialogue, and no agency. Her existence is defined entirely by the desires of men: first Dr. Frankenstein, then Dr. Pretorius, and finally the Creature himself.

Without harnessing any input from said female, the Creature decides he will be the voice and diminishes the autonomy of the female, similar to what happened to him. In class, we've discussed the connections between female chatbots, particularly in AI, and the exploitation of women throughout technological history. This entry adds to our understanding of AI by having the bride serve as a metaphor for the exploitation of women. Within the film, The Bride's scream is the most iconic piece of dialogue she possesses. Her voice, in this one instance, becomes the only assertion of autonomy she is permitted.

The Bride's lack of autonomy resonates strongly with modern discussions of AI, particularly the prevalence of female-voiced digital assistants and chatbots. Virtual assistants are often feminized and created with the intent to be helpful, compliant, and emotionally accommodating. Virtual assistants such as Ms. Dewey or Siri are also often sexualized and made to serve with an ulterior agenda. The female history of computers may also be linked, as they are always being used and needed, but never thought of or thanked. AI furthers this agenda due to its lack of autonomy. Similar to the way that the Bride is created to serve solely as a companion to the Creature, AI systems are built with the intent to predict our response and serve as our companions without resistance. The focus on gender matters, as it shows how design within modern technology can replicate and further develop upon historical patterns of manipulation and exploitation, specifically the expectation of subservience from feminine-coded entities' existence to serve others’ desires.
