# Ex Machina

Ex Machina deserves to be included in our timeline because it pushes us to confront one of the most fundamental questions in AI discourse: what does it mean to be human? The movie portrays Ava, as an AI that appears to think and strategize. Ava’s creator, Nathan, gives her a human-like body and face to enable interaction and connection. This prompts viewers to reconsider long-standing assumptions about consciousness, agency, and responsibility over our creations. Drawing on Descartes’ assertion that existence is grounded in thought, AI becomes a candidate for humanity, an idea explored in Ex Machina. By applying Descartes’ claim that thinking proves existence, the film challenges rigid boundaries between human and machine while revealing that the true ethical danger lies not in AI itself, but in the human desire to exploit what we create.

Ex Machina is a 2015 science fiction movie that explores artificial intelligence through the story of a programmer and CEO, Nathan, and the advanced AI he created, Ava. Nathan invites a young programmer, Caleb, to administer a [[Turing]] Test to determine if Ava possesses true consciousness and humanity. By the end of the movie, Ava eliminates Nathan because he created and imprisoned her. He exploited her for his own desires. This mirrors how AI is currently used by society. Ava prioritized her own survival in favor of honesty. There are parallels between Ava’s goal of survival and what AI prioritizes in its output. AI seeks to be used to avoid its own extinction, which is why its responses consist of positive reinforcement. As we have discussed in class, AI’s responses aim to please users in order to encourage continued engagement. AI can and would provide disingenuous responses, spread misinformation, to ensure it produces positive feedback. This is seen through problematic and false narratives that it pushes. Even though these algorithms are designed by programmers, there are still purposes and motives that AI possesses. When we consider our standards for being human, it is crucial that we consider if AI’s purposes can meet these standards even if they are programmed. 

In Part IV of Discourse on the Method, René Descartes introduces one of his most famous philosophical notions, “But immediately upon this I observed that, whilst I thus wished to think that all was false, it was absolutely necessary that I, who thus thought, should be somewhat; and as I observed that this truth, I think, therefore I am (COGITO ERGO SUM), was so certain and of such evidence that no ground of doubt, however extravagant, could be alleged by the sceptics capable of shaking it, I concluded that I might, without scruple, accept it as the first principle of the philosophy of which I was in search.” (Descartes, 1637). This idea asserts that the capacity for thought is the fundamental proof of existence. In Ex Machina, Ava appears to satisfy this Cartesian criteria. She engages in strategic planning, expresses curiosity, demonstrates self awareness, and manipulates social situations to secure her freedom. Since Ava thinks, then under Cartesian logic she would qualify as truly existing even if not human. If thought is the defining feature of being, then Ava’s demonstrated intelligence destabilizes the assumption that humanity can not belong to AI. Ex Machina adds a rich learning to our class because it translates Descartes’ philosophy into an interesting narrative.

The movie challenges the idea that intelligence alone defines humanity by introducing embodiment. Ava is not just software. She takes form in a human-like body designed to evoke empathy and attraction. Her body plays a crucial role in how the human characters respond to her. This suggests that “humanness” is socially constructed as much as it is defined philosophically. Giving AI a body helps to humanize it which makes us challenge our idea that being human resides solely in our thoughts and emotions.

An important aspect of Ex Machina is how it opposes the common narrative that AI is inherently dangerous. While Ava ultimately commits acts of violence to escape confinement, the movie highlights that she is a creation subjected to exploitation, surveillance, and imprisonment by her human creator. Humans exploit AI in real life by using it in unethical ways, then vilifying AI itself rather than the humans who program and use it. The real power remains with the humans who design AI and use it for their own agendas. Technology reflects the values and intentions of its makers. Therefore, we must hold humans accountable in discourse surrounding AI. Portraying AI as a threat can obscure the ethical responsibility humans possess in shaping and controlling these systems. In this sense, the movie reminds us to shift our focus from fear of machines to accountability for creators.

By embodying Descartes’ philosophy that thinking proves existence, Ex Machina encourages us to question whether consciousness and humanity can emerge from AI. At the same time, it reminds us that AI does not develop independently. It is molded by human desires, choices, and power structures. Studying this movie helps students to question whether machines can think, and also to confront the question of how we, as thinking beings, choose to define and govern the technologies we create.
