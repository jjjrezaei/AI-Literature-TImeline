# <i>2001: A Space Odyssey</i> (1968)

<p>Set in an alternate timeline in a more technologically advanced third millennium, <i>2001: A Space Odyssey</i>, a sci-fi film directed by Stanley Kubrick and co-writer Arthur C. Clarke, revolves around a spacecraft sent on a mission to Jupiter after uncovering a strange black rectangular object of unknown origin on the moon. The mission is primarily led by astronauts David Bowman, Frank Poole, and their sentient supercomputer HAL 9000.</p>

<p>This film is known for its groundbreaking portrayal of space travel and human-technology relationships, but it is especially known for featuring one of the most prominent AI antagonists in sci-fi literature and cinema. The <b>H</b>euristically Programmed <b>Al</b>gorithmic Computer (HAL 9000), or simply Hal, is an intelligent supercomputer that serves as the spacecraft’s “brain and central nervous system” (<i>2001</i> 1:01:38). A product of advanced machine intelligence, he is capable of “reproduc[ing] … most of the activities of the human brain … with incalculably greater speed and reliability,” (1:01:04) allowing him to think and communicate with the ship’s crew at a human-like level.</p>

<p><i>2001: A Space Odyssey</i> is an important addition to the timeline because it explores the fear of AI stemming from technology advancing beyond human understanding. It is especially relevant considering we have the tools to build AI models that closely resemble humans, but privacy concerns arise from this due to how AI companies are using consumer data (including personal information) and data scraped from the Internet to train their models, usually without their consent or knowledge.</p>

<p><h1>The Lip-Reading Scene</h1></p>
<p>As the film shows, advancements in artificial intelligence may come with potential privacy risks. In <a href="https://youtu.be/QFSE4dUJYM8?t=232">this scene</a>, David Bowman and Frank Poole enter a pod to discuss a suspected malfunction in Hal’s programming, and they consider disconnecting Hal by “cut[ting] his higher brain functions without disturbing the purely automatic and regulatory systems” vital to the mission (<i>2001</i> 1:26:24). Later in the scene, the view suddenly cuts to Hal’s perspective where he exclusively focuses on Dave and Frank’s mouths to follow their conversation despite not being able to hear them (1:27:00). As Hal focuses on the astronauts’ mouths, he gathers data from their conversation through his camera in real time and interprets it solely from their lip movements, similarly to how people with hearing loss read lips to understand speech. This demonstrates Hal’s ability to understand conversations without relying on sound, and the information he gathers is what drives him to murder Frank in space and the hibernating crew members on board and lock Dave out in an attempt to stop him from disconnecting him.</p>

<p>The different perspectives in this scene are important. When the camera is situated <a href="https://youtu.be/QFSE4dUJYM8?t=232">inside the pod</a> (<i>2001</i> 1:24:24-1:27:00), Kubrick evokes the feeling of sitting in a pod with both David and Frank, allowing the viewer to listen to their private conversation both visually and sonically. When the view cuts to <a href="https://youtu.be/QFSE4dUJYM8?t=387">Hal's perspective</a> (1:27:00-22), Kubrick places the viewer into the eyes of an AI training itself on a conversation it cannot hear (as evidenced by all sound being replaced by ambience during a close-up of Hal's eye and his POV seconds later), but can still see. Kubrick illustrates these privacy risks by providing an extreme close-up of the lips of David and Frank as a way for Hal to spy on their conversation without giving himself away, unbeknownst to the astronauts inside the pod.</p>

<p>This particular scene matters to our understanding of artificial intelligence because it relates to the training of AI models (large language models, image generation, etc.) in the current decade. We train AI models by feeding it different kinds of information like text, audio, and/or images. When we give an AI “vision,” or the ability to see, it processes visual data from the continuous flow of images being fed to it and translates it into data that it can learn from and train on. For example, in 2016, researchers at Oxford University <a href="https://gizmodo.com/researchers-just-created-the-most-amazing-lip-reading-s-1788748163">developed a software that can read lips</a> at “93.4 percent accuracy, compared to just 52 percent accuracy achieved by an experienced human lip reader.” (Liszewski) This shows that with continuous training and taking how these models are built into consideration, these models could potentially exceed human ability. Bringing this statistic in relation to <i>2001</i>, it begs the following questions: <i>will technology advance to the point where artificial intelligence surpasses humans in <b>all</b> aspects? How long do we have until our creations gain omniscience and (potentially) revolt against us? Is omniscience the end of privacy?</i></p>
<p>Ultimately, <i>2001</i>’s lip-reading scene can be interpreted as a warning about the potential privacy concerns regarding technological advancements in the third millennium. When an AI like the HAL 9000 trains itself on human interaction and what it sees, that kind of training is usually done without our consent or knowledge. Today, big tech companies like Microsoft, Google, and xAI are constantly pushing people into trying their latest AI models that they claim can "think" (Google Gemini), "see" (Microsoft Copilot Vision), and analyze videos (xAI's Grok). Meanwhile, anti-AI consumers believe that these are just a ploy to collect even more of their personal data. <i>2001: A Space Odyssey</i> offers a cautionary tale about scientific progress, where technological advancements and unchecked AI can lead to tragic outcomes.</p>
