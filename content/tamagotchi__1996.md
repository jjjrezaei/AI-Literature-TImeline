# Tamagotchi (1996)

</p> <p>Tamagotchi was released by Bandai (a Japanese company) in 1996 and quickly became popular around the world. It was a small handheld digital pet that users had to take care of by feeding it, cleaning it, and checking on it throughout the day. The Tamagotchi had simple programmed needs like hunger and happiness, and if users ignored it, it could get sick or even “die.” Even though it was very basic and did not have real intelligence, many people became emotionally attached to it. Users often felt guilty if they forgot to take care of it, which shows how strongly people reacted to something that was just a digital program in everyday life.</p> <p>Tamagotchi is important to the history of AI because it shows that machines do not need to be advanced to feel meaningful or “alive.” The device does not think, learn, or understand anything, but it still creates the feeling of a relationship. Sherry Turkle explains that people are drawn to “the illusion of companionship without the demands of friendship” (Turkle 1). This quote helps explain why Tamagotchi worked so well. The digital pet cannot actually care about its user, but it is designed to act like it does, and that illusion is enough for people to feel emotionally connected to it.</p> <p>This idea also connects to <em>Frankenstein</em> by Mary Shelley, where Walton says, “I have no friend, Margaret… I bitterly feel the want of a friend” (Shelley 54), showing how strong the desire for companionship is. This helps explain why people are drawn to things like Tamagotchi, which offer a simple form of connection. In the novel, the creature is intelligent and capable of feeling, but it is rejected by humans because it looks unnatural and is seen as artificial. This suggests that what matters is not just intelligence, but how people respond to something they see as non-human. In contrast, Tamagotchi shows the opposite situation. Even though it has no real intelligence, people still accept and care for it. This comparison shows that emotional connection to artificial life depends more on human perception than on the actual abilities of the machine.</p> <p>This example also challenges the idea that AI develops in a straight line toward becoming more intelligent or more human-like. Turkle writes that “we are lonely but fearful of intimacy” (Turkle 1), which helps explain why people turn to technology for connection. Tamagotchi fits this idea because it provides a simple and controlled form of connection that feels safe. This shows that the importance of AI is not only about how advanced it is, but also about how people use it to meet emotional needs.</p> <p>Tamagotchi also blurs the line between real life and simulation. By making users responsible for its well-being, it encourages people to treat a non-living object as if it were alive. This kind of interaction continues in later technologies like robotic pets and AI companions, but Tamagotchi shows that these ideas started much earlier and with much simpler systems. It helped normalize the idea that humans can form emotional relationships with machines.</p> <p>Tamagotchi deserves a place on the AI timeline because it shifts the focus away from technological progress and toward human behavior. It shows that AI is not just about building smarter systems, but about how people give meaning to those systems. By including Tamagotchi, we can see that the history of AI is not only about innovation and advancement, but also about emotion, perception, and everyday interaction. This shows that AI has never been just about becoming more intelligent over time, but has always depended on human imagination and emotion to feel real, which challenges the idea that AI is always improving over time.</p> <p><strong>Works Cited</strong></p> <p>Shelley, Mary. <em>Frankenstein</em>. 1818.
