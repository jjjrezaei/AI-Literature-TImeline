# The Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence (1956)

<p>The Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence took place in the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College in New Hampshire. It is often called the official beginning of artificial intelligence as a scientific field. In their 1955 proposal, John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon introduced the term “artificial intelligence.” They claimed that “every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it” (McCarthy et al.). This sentence assumes that intelligence can be described precisely enough to be translated into computational rules.</p> <p>The proposal itself outlined several research problems that the group hoped to explore during the workshop. McCarthy and his colleagues believed that processes such as learning, reasoning, language use, and problem solving could be studied and eventually reproduced by computers. They suggested that machines might be able to improve their own performance, form abstract concepts, or solve problems that previously required human intelligence. The goal of the summer workshop was to bring together researchers who could collaborate on these questions and begin developing methods for studying machine intelligence. By framing intelligence as a set of research problems, the proposal treated thinking as something that could be broken down into smaller parts that scientists could study systematically.</p> <p>Dartmouth did not invent the idea of artificial life or intelligent machines. Long before 1956, people had imagined artificial life in myths, philosophy, and literature. What Dartmouth did was turn these older ideas into a formal research program. The organizers defined intelligence in a specific way. They treated thinking as something that could be described, broken into steps, and processed by a computer. As Margaret Boden writes, “Artificial intelligence (AI) seeks to make computers do the sorts of things that minds can do,” describing intelligence as a set of “information-processing capacities” (Boden 1). In this model, intelligence becomes mechanical and rule-based. Emotion, the body, and social experience are not the focus.</p> <p>The people involved in the proposal were also important to the development of computing and AI. John McCarthy later became one of the founders of artificial intelligence and created the programming language LISP, which became widely used in early AI research. Marvin Minsky went on to help establish major AI laboratories at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Claude Shannon had already become famous for developing information theory, which helped establish the mathematical foundations of digital communication and computing. By bringing together these influential researchers, the Dartmouth workshop helped turn AI from a speculative idea into a legitimate research field.</p> <p>Dartmouth itself also became an important site in the early history of computing. Hosting the workshop connected the university to emerging networks of researchers who would continue to shape artificial intelligence research in universities and laboratories across the United States. The conference helped create collaborations and institutions that would guide AI research for decades.</p> <p>Including Dartmouth on our timeline challenges the idea that AI is the natural result of steady technological progress. AI was not inevitable. It began with a decision about what intelligence means. By defining intelligence as rule-based and computational, the Dartmouth organizers shaped the direction of AI research for decades. Other ways of understanding intelligence, such as emotional or embodied thinking, were pushed aside. Dartmouth marks the moment when one definition of intelligence gained power, funding, and authority.</p> <p>This early claim also helps explain why many stories about AI focus on fear. If thinking can be separated from the human body and recreated in machines, then machines might one day act beyond human control. Both the excitement and the anxiety surrounding AI grow from this original assumption that intelligence can be simulated.</p> <p>Dartmouth does not mark the natural birth of artificial intelligence. It marks the historical moment when intelligence was redefined as something engineers believed they could build. Recognizing this moment helps us see that AI is not simply the result of faster computers or inevitable progress. Instead, it developed from specific historical decisions about how scientists understood the human mind and how machines might attempt to reproduce human intelligence.</p>
