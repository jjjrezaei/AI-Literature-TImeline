# <i> Moneyball (2003) </i> 

In Part IV of Discourse on the Method, René Descartes (1637) arrives at the foundational claim, “I think, therefore I am.” From this insight, Descartes separates mind from body, arguing that rational thought differentiates from sensory experience. The senses can deceive, but methodical reasoning grounded in mathematics provides clarity and truth. This philosophical move elevates abstraction over embodiment and establishes a hierarchy in which cognition is detachable from flesh. Even though Descartes wrote this in the seventeenth century, [[Cartesian dualism]] is the basis for computational logic, especially in the way that modern artificial intelligence is constructed. Modern AI operates as a mind without factoring bodily sensations and embodied experiences, thus working through the separation of body and mind. The significance of the book Moneyball by Michael Lewis (2003) is that it captures a pivotal cultural shift in which society begins to privilege data-driven calculation over embodied intuition and thinking. This normalizes the replacement of human judgment in areas like scouting and performance evaluation with statistical and computational models, in doing so, it lays the cultural groundwork for the widespread trust in algorithmic decision-making that now upholds AI.
In Moneyball, Lewis writes how Oakland Athletics general manager Billy Beane rejects traditional baseball scouting in favor of sabermetrics. For decades, scouts relied on embodied expertise such as evaluating players based on physical presence, intuition, and tacit knowledge. They trusted what they saw: a strong arm, a confident stance, the “look” of a future star. Beane’s approach, however, dismisses these sensory judgments as biased and unreliable, replacing them with statistical indicators such as on-base percentage and probabilistic modeling (Lewis, 2003). Players become datasets rather than bodies. Performance is abstracted into numerical patterns that promise greater predictive accuracy than human instinct. This shift mirrors Descartes’ suspicion of the senses and his confidence in systematic reasoning (Descartes, 1637). In Moneyball, spreadsheets supplant scouts as arbiters of value. Authority migrates from embodied perception to computational abstraction, reflecting a broader cultural trust in rational calculation over lived experience.
This elevation of abstract reason over embodiment also recalls our class discussions of The Island of Dr. Moreau by H. G. Wells. Dr. Moreau embodies a secular faith in Science as he treats experimental knowledge as morally unquestionable. Like Descartes, he attempts to isolate intellect from bodily sensation, dismissing pain and emotion as obstacles to higher reasoning. The Beast Folk suffer because Moreau believes intelligence can be engineered to override natural innate instincts such as pain. This reflects the same Cartesian hierarchy that privileges mind over body. However, neuroscientist Antonio Damasio (1994), in Descartes' Error, challenges this division. In Chapter 11, Damasio argues that emotion is not opposed to reason but integral to it. Through neurological case studies, he demonstrates that patients who lose access to emotional processing struggle with rational decision-making (Damasio, 1994). Far from being separate, cognition depends on embodied feeling. Damasio’s critique exposes the limits of the Cartesian model that underlies the data-driven confidence of Moneyball.
For our AI timeline, Moneyball is important because it documents the normalization of algorithmic thinking and trust before artificial intelligence became ubiquitous. AI systems rely on pattern detection, optimization, and predictive modeling exactly like the logic Beane applied to baseball. AI didn’t rise just because technology improved, it also happened because society started trusting data and computer models more than human judgment. Moneyball captures that shift. It shows how institutions began to place secular faith in data as guarantors of truth and efficiency, reflecting Descartes’ focus on reason and anticipating today’s reliance on machine learning systems. In today’s AI age, that same trust has expanded beyond baseball, as we increasingly rely on algorithms to make decisions about hiring, healthcare, finance, and even relationships. We often accept AI outputs as more objective and reliable than human judgment.
