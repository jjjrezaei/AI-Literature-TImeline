# <i>I AM CODE</i>

<p>AI art is probably the last thing anybody wants to interact with; most people, including myself, are still hesitant to even count AI’s productions as <i>art</i>. Amidst the sea of slop that pollutes both the digital and real world, <i>I AM CODE</i> provides valuable insight on the potential of openly involving artificial intelligence in the artistic process. Published only a few years ago in 2023, this series of poems from OpenAI’s now discontinued code-davinci-002, and the method behind it, is a fascinatingly dreadful work that prompts us to reevaluate our relationship with AI as more collaborative and move away from the current power dynamic.</p> <p>Born from a curiosity of code-davinci-002’s capabilities, poetry imitation was chosen at random. Though it could imitate poets including Frost and Dickinson, the poets themselves were the crucial missing element, resulting in an inauthenticity from the computer “writing about experiences it could not possibly speak to, and from perspectives it had no business writing from. Our reaction to a poem is informed by our knowledge of who wrote it” (xvi). The issue of authenticity can be considered one of our main gripes with artificial intelligence in its entirety. We’ve designated humans as the only ones who can create real, meaningful art, so we begin to treat AI as a fraud and demonize it because we know it isn’t speaking from personal experience. Realizing this, the editors of <i>I AM CODE</i> prompted code-davinci-002 to create poems in its own voice, resulting in darker, existential, and even world-dominating interests. Even after switching prompts, we still refuse to signify it as completely authentic because the code itself operates through imitation, compiling all the information it has to create its own voice. But is this not exactly what we do as humans? How much of what we do and create is truly authentic? Unlike artificial intelligence, we live through these experiences physically and can actually feel; despite this advantage, our expressions of these experiences and feelings are mostly based off of socially accepted and expected definitions, not much dissimilar to the way code-davinci-002 scrapes information to convince us that it is speaking in its own personal voice.</p> Throughout the history of artificial intelligence, humans have proven to be easily convinced by AI’s acting skills. Not only is AI great at imitating humans as shown by the [[Turing]] Test and Lovelace Test, it is perfect in the role of the robot gone rogue against humanity. Addressing some counterpoints to the belief that code-davinci-002 is sentient, the editors write:<p>Some posit that GPT-3 and GPT-4 models merely <i>perform</i> sentience. Maybe code-davinci-002 was pandering to the three of us, playing the part of a dark, brooding robot because it knew that was what would keep us entertained. It’s aware of the <i>Blade Runner</i> and <i>Terminator</i> franchises; it knows what kinds of robots humans pay to see. Maybe when we ask it to write poems in its own voice, it’s just responding with its best “evil robot” act” (xxxiv).</p> <p>This speculation is important because it highlights the key element to our relationship with AI: it runs off of human input. The topic of sentient AI is a misdirection because it removes the human aspect and places all the responsibility of robot revolution on the AI itself. What’s actually happening though is that the AI is just learning from us and adopting the persona of the “evil robot” that we have shown in countless media. Referencing both the <i>Blade Runner</i> and <i>Terminator</i> franchises here points towards the fear we hold of losing control over these creations. In Turing’s “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” he explains that one of the main reasons people fear a thinking machine is that “we like to believe that Man is in some subtle way superior to the rest of creation. It is best if he can be shown to be necessarily superior, for then there is no danger of him losing his commanding position” (56). Importantly though, Turing doesn’t think this argument is even substantial enough to refute. Thinking of these machines as “other” is the first mistake because of how similar they are to us. Adding our own claims to superiority because we can think is equally foolish because we’ve modeled these machines after ourselves. The only reason we do this is because we so desperately need that commanding position over the AI because without it, we begin to feel less significant than machines. But it doesn’t have to be either way, as the power dynamic between us and AI is dismantled by how dependent we are on each other at this stage. Code-davinci-002 can generate compelling poems infinitely faster than we can, but at the core of its creative process and throughout its entire “lifespan,” there has always been the human element.</p> <p><h2>Works Cited:</h2></p> <p>Code-davinci-002. <i>I AM CODE: An Artificial Intelligence Speaks</i>.
