# <i>Galatea</i> , the bridge to the Post-Humanities

While interacting with Galatea we got to see multiple perspectives and conclusions, many of them ending in a confusing and illusionist point of view. Galatea was nothing but a smoke screen, an imitation, a game. Alan Turing explores this idea in his [[Turing]] Test, where machine and human are put to the test to identify who is who, more importantly if humans can differentiate machines. Galatea is a crucial example of the Turing Test, where humans interact with an NPC that records the conversation and continues it, resulting in some interesting outcomes. Galatea is the bridge between humans and machines, where instead of replacing human interaction we learn to coexist and nurture each other, not denying the machines or humans being taken over.
<p><i>Alan Turing’s</i> imitation game was a revolutionary piece of literature, where he argues how machines are able to imitate human interaction and emotion. The only way they are capable of doing this is because they are already made this way. “The reader must accept it as a fact that digital computers can be constructed, and indeed have been constructed, according to the principles we have described, and that they can in fact mimic the actions of a human computer very closely.” (Turing 53). Galatea is an example of this, it was already made to mimic the actions of a human computer, one who takes information in and creates a possible scenario to play out for the player. This connection between machine and human becomes stronger because of how Galatea was made, she was made to interact directly with what the player says or asks. The actions that can be done are also very intimate and personal, hugging, kissing, hitting. However the player that is interacting with Galatea grows interest and attachment to her.</p> <p>The bond between Galatea and the main character is unique and interesting. Although there are people controlling the conversation, it is just between Galatea and the man seeing her in the museum. The fear of machines replacing humans and jobs is growing quickly, the pushback towards AI is growing everyday, but if we take a step back and think about how humans and machines can benefit from this interaction. “<i>Galatea 2.2</i> foregrounds affect as a key dimension of human–machine interaction. The protagonist invests the artificial intelligence with emotional significance, projecting desire, grief, and longing onto it. The machine becomes a screen for unresolved human emotions, challenging the assumption that affect belongs exclusively to human subjects.” (Powers 5). <i>Galatea 2.2</i> offers the idea that the humanities can overpower the fear of machines taking over. Human-machine interaction is crucial to understand AI and to understand ourselves. Putting all this emotion onto a computer and having it reflect to us is not what a mathematical machine would do. The idea is to coexist, to learn that neither machine nor human can overpower the other. AI and computers are more advanced than ever, having it imitate human experience and language is the first step into human-machine symbiosis.</p> <p><h2>Work Cited</h2></p> <p>Powers, Richard. Galatea 2.2. Macmillan, 1995.</p> <p>Wardrip-Fruin, Noah, and Nick Montfort. The New Media Reader. Cambridge, Mass., MIT Press, 2003.</p>
