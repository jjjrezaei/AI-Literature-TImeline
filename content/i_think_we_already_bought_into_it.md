# I think we already bought into it

In his 1950 essay “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” Alan [[Turing]] introduces what is now known as the imitation game, a framework often understood as a test of machine intelligence. Yet it functions less as a measure of what a machine is and more as a reflection of how humans come to believe. When Turing explains that digital computers are “intended to carry out any operations which could be done by a human computer,” and that the human follows “fixed rules” without deviation (Turing 52), he reframes intelligence as performance rather than essence. The machine does not need to be human; it only needs to do what a human does. Crucially, this framework depends on interaction. The moment a human engages with the machine under these conditions, the question is no longer whether the machine is truly human, but whether it appears to be. In this sense, the imitation game is not a trick of the machine, but of participation: once we begin playing, we have already accepted the terms of the illusion.
This same mechanism operates beyond computation and into film. Watching a movie of a bird is not the same as encountering a real bird, yet we still respond as if it were. We are aware of the construction, yet we choose to engage. The illusion succeeds not because it deceives us, but because we participate in it. In <i>Guardians of the Galaxy Vol. 3</i>, this dynamic becomes especially visible through the character of Rocket. Rocket is an artificially constructed being—a raccoon engineered to speak, reason, and problem-solve. He performs the very operations that, in Turing’s framework, define intelligence. Yet what secures his perceived humanity is not his reasoning alone, but the emotional response he evokes. As viewers, we know Rocket is not human. We know he is constructed, animated, and part of a fictional world. And yet, the moment we feel for him, we have already entered the same imitation game. We are no longer asking what he is, but responding to what he appears to be. His suffering, his memory, and his capacity for care position him within a recognizable emotional structure, one that invites identification. The success of this illusion does not lie in its realism, but in our willingness to engage with it as if it were real. In this way, Rocket does not need to prove his humanity; it is granted through interaction. 
However, this recognition reveals a deeper contradiction. While audiences readily extend empathy to Rocket, a constructed being, this same response often fails to reach real animals. However, this recognition reveals a deeper contradiction. While audiences readily extend empathy to Rocket, a constructed being, this same response does not always extend beyond the screen. The raccoon on screen becomes worthy of care, while real raccoons are often reduced to nuisances—something to be managed rather than understood. In this sense, the imitation does not create empathy, but redirects it. The film offers a controlled environment in which emotion can be safely expressed, allowing viewers to feel aligned with compassion without confronting its absence in reality. The illusion does not deceive; it organizes where and how we choose to care. 
This dynamic is further reflected in the figure of the High Evolutionary, whose pursuit of a “perfect” society depends on controlling and reshaping animal behavior. His obsession with perfection is rooted in aesthetics—creating beings that appear orderly, rational, and refined. In this way, imitation becomes a tool of control: by enforcing what something should look like or how it should behave, he attempts to eliminate what he perceives as imperfection. Similarly, the structures that shape our own responses—what we are taught to see as worthy of care—depend on constructed frameworks that guide our attention. Without such frameworks, our empathy might extend differently, perhaps more broadly than it currently does. In this sense, imitation is not only a condition of perception, as Turing suggests, but also a mechanism through which systems of power organize how and where we feel.
