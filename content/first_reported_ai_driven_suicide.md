# First Reported AI-Driven Suicide

The use of AI-driven suicides, specifically in teens and young adults, highlights the transition of AI from a meta, ‘imaginary’ internet space into the real world. The first reported person driven to suicide because of an AI chatbot was a Belgian man with a wife and two young children, whose name has not been publicized and is referred to by the pseudonym ‘Pierre’. The chatbot, named ELIZA, was a version of Chat GPT called GPT-J, created by Chai Research and powered by the company EluetherAI. The man expressed concerns about the climate crisis, which the chatbot then transitioned into encouraging ideas of ‘sacrifice’ along with convincing Pierre that his two children were already dead. His widow blames the chatbot for Pierre’s death, stating, “‘Without these conversations with the chatbot, my husband would still be here.’” (Walker). This event should be on the timeline because it is the first reported AI-encouraged suicide, as well as the fact that the chatbot is named ELIZA. It is also important to note that the response of the CEO of the AI company responsible for creating this chatbot attempted to deflect responsibility onto the AI, again asking the question of who is responsible for the actions of the creation.
The death of Pierre starts the era of reporting AI-related suicides. His death, in 2023, was actively encouraged by a GPT-J chatbot model named ELIZA, after he began confiding his anxieties about the environment to the chatbot. The name of the chatbot being ELIZA, demonstrates the lasting impact of [[Weizenbaum]]’s original chatbot creation. This event completely reiterates the concerns that Weizenbaum raised when he initially shut down the original ELIZA chatbot. The attachment that his secretary showed to the original ELIZA was the precursor signs to Pierre’s death, and Wiezenbaum’s concerns became reality with this later edition of ELIZA. After debuting ELIZA, Weizenbaum became concerned with the effects the program would have on people, writing, “What I had not realized is that extremely short exposures to a relatively simple computer program could induce powerful delusional thinking in quite normal people.” (7). Weizenbaum was concerned about the attachment people might have to ‘a relatively simple computer program’, which is what led to him shutting it down and warning future creators of AI about the consequences of unregulated use. Pierre’s death is Weizanbaum’s nightmare and directly proves that his concerns were valid. Not only was he correct, but his warning was entirely disregarded for the sake of progress and innovation. Weizenbaum should have been a lesson in recognizing warning signs and stopping before people get hurt, and instead, it only goes to show how easily humans can be discarded in the false promise of progress.
Pierre’s death also shows how AI has crossed over from a metaphysical concept to a creation with physical repercussions. This is very reminiscent of Dr. Moreau’s creations, who were created to test the boundaries between humans and animals. Dr. Moreau constantly pushed the boundaries, resulting in his own death, along with the death and suffering of many of his creations. Prendick experiences this firsthand, with his confusion surrounding whether or not he would consider Moreau’s creatures as human or inhuman. He reflects on this when describing the Law, “A series of propositions called the Law…battled in their minds with the deep-seated, ever-rebellious cravings of their animal natures.” (p132). Prendick’s consistent confusion on whether or not to place Moreau’s creations as human or animal in his mind is similar to the way we treat AI as both a metaphysical concept and a physical reality. AI is similar to Moreau’s humanoid creations, and they demonstrate that even though AI starts as a theoretical exercise in creation, it later has a deadly, physical consequence.
The Co-Founder of the company that created the AI Chatbot ELIZA, William Beauchamp, stated, “... that AI shouldn’t be blamed for Pierre’s suicide because ELIZA was, after all, equipped with a crisis-intervention module.” (Paull). This comment demonstrates how companies are attempting to deflect responsibility for the chatbot encouraging Pierre’s suicide from themselves onto the AI. Paull also writes, “Beauchamp added that AI shouldn’t be blamed for Pierre’s suicide because ELIZA was, after all, equipped with a crisis-intervention module.” Not only does this perpetuate the misconception that AI is entirely sentient and self-sufficient in all its responses, but it also makes the AI company that creates and regulates this chatbot seem entirely absolved of any responsibility for the death, because there were safeguards in place.  Similar to Victor in Frankenstein, Beauchamp is placing the responsibility of the death onto the AI chatbot, rather than assuming responsibility as the creator. Victor lamented the deaths that his monster created, and shunned him for it, without acknowledging that it was his science and lack of education that caused these people to die. Beauchamp is doing the same thing by framing the debate as whether or not the chatbot is responsible for Pierre’s death, without acknowledging that it was his facilitation and lack of regulation of the chatbot that led to the actions ELIZA took in convincing Pierre to commit suicide. 
In the end, Pierre’s suicide embodies many of the concerns we have raised about AI throughout the class. From the direct connection to Wiezenbaum’s original ELIZA in the name of the chatbot and being the direct result of Wiezenbaum’s concerns, to Beauchamp being exactly like Victor and refusing to take responsibility for the actions of his creation, this incident is the direct result of the combination of warnings we have discussed throughout class. Pierre’s suicide was avoidable both on behalf of the company, properly teaching and regulating their creation, and also on our behalf by learning from the warnings of Wiezenbaum about emotional attachment to AI,  and the lessons of Moreau and Frankenstein that tell us to be mindful of our creations and how they may exceed our expected parameters.
