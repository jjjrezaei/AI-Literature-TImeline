# Thus Spoke Zarathustra

Friedrich Nietzsche’s Thus Spoke Zarathustra is an 1883 philosophical novel that is a relevant addition to our timeline because its core questions about creation, values, and human purpose mirror today’s discussions regarding AI. Given its 1883 publication, Thus Spoke Zarathustra might seem far removed from AI and modern technology. However, its core ideas, such as Übermensch and Last Men, connect to modern AI usage. The novel explores fundamental questions that AI forces us to face, including who creates values, what counts as truth, and what humanity is becoming in the face of its own creations.

In Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Nietzsche explains his concept of an Übermensch (Overman/Superman) is an ideal human who creates their own values and purpose rather than following society’s rules. The Overman is someone who thinks for themselves, embraces challenges, continually growing and improving. Nietzsche writes, “The Übermensch is the creator of values, and he alone can say yes to life without appealing to God or tradition.” (Nietzsche 25). This title represents someone who actively shapes their own life and meaning, rather than passively accepting what others believe. In the context of AI, this becomes important because AI systems do not have values of their own. AI systems reflect the values of the people who design and train them. This means humans are in a position similar to Nietzsche’s ideal by shaping the frameworks that influence how others think, decide, and understand the world. AI gives humans the opportunity and responsibility to design systems that influence how others think, act, and understand the world. This gives programmers and society a kind of power associated with Übermensch. It is whether we do this thoughtfully or carelessly that brings up a dilemma. Conversely, if humans engage with AI irresponsibly, allowing AI to do all their thinking and creating, then AI could be interpreted as a digital successor to the Übermensch, that redefines what is “true” or “good”. In the case that humans allow AI to complete their thinking and creating, we risk becoming Nietzsche’s concept of Last Men, serving as the complete opposite to the Übermensch. 

In Alan [[Turing]]’s “Computing Machinery and Intelligence”, he highlights that intelligence is not about fixed knowledge, it is about the ability to develop and create. He writes, “Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child’s?” (Turing 62). Rather than making a machine that mimics a fully developed adult mind, Turing suggests that we should create one that starts like a child’s mind being flexible, curious, and able to learn and grow. Much like a child learns through experience rather than by having everything pre-programmed, we should create machines through this lens. This relates to Nietzsche’s concept of the Übermensch because both emphasize creative potential and transformation beyond existing limits. Nietzsche’s Übermensch is not confined by traditional norms, he creates new values and ways of being. Similarly, Turing’s suggestion to simulate a child’s mind highlights open mindedness, learning, and the capacity for growth, rather than replicating fixed adult intelligence. In both cases, the focus is on development, self-overcoming, and creation of new ideas, whether in humans or in AI.

The antithesis to the Übermensch is Nietzsche’s concept of  Last Men. This refers to humans that prioritize comfort, safety, and convenience. The Last Men avoid risk, struggle, and ambition, preferring ease over all else. Nietzsche explains in reference to the Last Men, “‘The earth will be small for him, and he will not grow any higher.’ He will have no danger, no thirst for adventure, no heights, no beyonds.” (Nietzsche 32). These people don’t continue to strive for growth and instead settle for stagnation. Nietzsche uses them as a warning. In an age where AI is accessible and offers convenience over effort, the risk of becoming Last Men heightens. For example, instead of problem solving, people might let AI do the work. Instead of forming original opinions, humans might accept AI-generated answers. Instead of taking the time to properly understand and learn new concepts, humans might learn to choose quick, easy results. This creates a society where convenience replaces effort, which is what Nietzsche warned against. In this sense, AI can contribute to a Last Man mindset, where people become comfortable but less engaged or ambitious. The Last Men would abandon the valuable task of creation, giving the task of creation to AI. This brings up the valuable question of whether we are like Nietzsche’s Last Men, following systems that reinforce convenience and efficiency, or if we are building toward something transformative that reshapes human potential?

In Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Nietzsche declared that “God is dead.” (Nietzsche 108). This statement does not only pertain to religion. It refers to the collapse of absolute authorities and universal truths as sources of meaning. This leaves humans with the responsibility to create their own values and guide their own lives. In the context of AI, this idea is relevant because AI systems do not possess inherent morality, judgment, or understanding of truth. They operate entirely based on the algorithms and objectives humans provide. Without careful guidance, AI could reinforce biases, manipulate, or promote misinformation. This creates systems that seem informative but lack grounding in factual truth. AI mirrors Nietzsche’s idea that “God is Dead” and we don’t have a true standard for truth or goodness, we are constructing it.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra challenges us to consider who defines values, truth, and purpose. These questions are central to how we create and use AI. Nietzsche’s concepts of the Übermensch and the Last Men serve as warnings and guides. Humans can either thoughtfully shape AI to expand creativity and potential or passively rely on it, risking stagnation. Reflecting on these ideas, we are reminded that AI is not neutral, it amplifies the choices we make. Nietzsche’s novel emphasizes human responsibility in designing systems that shape society and our future.
